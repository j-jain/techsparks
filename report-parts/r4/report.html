Four things worth knowing before you choose

  • Six ideas hold up; none is a pure software company. The six with a business score of 3 are mostly services that use AI to do paid work faster: inspect a flat, file a registration, prepare a claim, quote a job. Section 5 explains why Indian versions of funded startups keep ending up that way.
  • If you want AI/ML to carry the product, the strongest are F2 (reading claims and quoting the policy), F4 (reading engineering drawings) and M1 (finding parasites on a slide). All three need a human to confirm the output, and each card explains why.
  • The best match between an idea and a mentor is one of yours. Vibhore Sharma's only public quote is about manufacturing being "slow, expensive, and hard to scale". Your factory-floor voice idea, and F4 and M3 in this report, all sit there. Section 6 has the full mapping, including the eight finalists from 3 November.
  • "Nobody does this in India" was wrong more often than right. The first round made that claim for seven ideas. A second search by name left it standing for one.

A way to pick, by team

  • AI/ML first, software only: F2 or F4.
  • Vision with a little hardware: M3 or P4.
  • A bio member leading: M1.
  • Someone who knows maps: P1.
  • The demo least likely to fail: F1's pack-label checker, or P4's area test.

How to read this report

3. Ideas read from the mentors' records

These four come from what the mentors' funds have already paid for. Each was designed in detail by one agent and its key facts were attacked by another. Two of the four shrank under that scrutiny; the cards say so.

M3 · Solder and placement inspector for small electronics assemblers

Buildable overnight 5/5 · Business 3/5 · AI/ML about 60% · Track: Frontier Hardware

  • The problem. India made electronics worth Rs 13.11 lakh crore in 2025–26 (MeitY figures, 24 Jul 2026). Big plants check boards with automatic optical inspection machines that cost about Rs 50–60 lakh (trade listings, snippet). Small assemblers cannot buy one, so a person checks each board by eye under a lamp, and faults reach the customer.
  • Who pays. The owner of a 20–200 person assembly unit. Today the cost is one to three inspectors plus rework and returns. A plausible price is Rs 30,000 for the station and Rs 1,500 a month. With about 4,000 such sites (assumption), that is roughly Rs 7 crore a year of recurring revenue: real, but small.
  • How it works.
    1. A fixed jig, an overhead camera and a ring light photograph each board.
    2. The image is aligned to a photo of a known-good board.
    3. The board's pick-and-place file says where each part should be, so the software cuts out one small image per part.
    4. Each cut-out gets three checks: an anomaly score against good boards, a solder-fault detector, and a polarity check.
    5. The screen shows red boxes and a pass or fail.
  • The AI/ML core.
    • Anomaly detection with PatchCore (Intel's open anomalib library). It trains only on photos of good boards, so a shop needs 20–50 good photos and no fault labels.
    • A YOLO detector fine-tuned on SolDef_AI, an open set of solder-fault images, to name the fault.
    • Round 1 pointed at the well-known PCB datasets. The design agent found those cover bare boards before assembly and cannot teach solder or placement faults.
    • What one top-down camera cannot see: joint height, joints hidden under chips, slightly lifted leads. It catches missing, wrong, shifted or reversed parts, solder bridges and gross solder faults.
  • Why India needs its own version. Price is 100 times lower than a machine. Small Indian shops run many board types in small batches, which suits learning from good boards better than a day of rule-writing per board. The operator screen should be in Hindi with pictures.
  • Funded analogue. SixSense, in Peak XV's Surge 11 cohort, sells no-code vision inspection to chip plants (Inc42, 25 Sep 2025). This idea keeps the method and changes the line, the price and the training.
  • Who has tried. SwitchOn raised $8 million in July 2026 (Entrepreneur India) and Frinks AI raised $5.4 million in May 2025 (Tribune). Both sell to larger plants. Nobody was found selling a station under Rs 50,000.
  • What you could show by noon. Solder 20 cheap through-hole kits: 10 good, 10 with one planted fault each (bridge, missing resistor, reversed LED, reversed capacitor, cold joint). Train on 30 photos of good boards only. The number: faults caught on the 10 faulty boards and false alarms on the 10 good ones, each board photographed three times after being put back in the jig (60 images). Compare with simple pixel differencing, and with a teammate inspecting each board for 30 seconds.
  • Parts and cost. A webcam or USB microscope camera (Rs 1,500–3,500), a ring light (Rs 400), kits (about Rs 2,000). About Rs 4,000–6,000 in all (estimates).
  • The hard question. "One flat camera misses half of solder defects. Is this a toy?" It misses joint-shape and hidden-joint faults. It replaces the eye-and-lamp check, not the Rs 50 lakh machine, and the pitch should say exactly that.
  • Suits. Vibhore Sharma (his one public quote is about manufacturing; his funds back CynLr and Unbox Robotics). Anuj Mehta (student-built hardware plus applied AI). Arshia Mal (WTFund backed a desktop pick-and-place machine).
  • Overlap with your own work. Same technique as your factory CCTV idea, in a different sector.

M1 · Phone-on-microscope slide reader for malaria

Buildable overnight 3/5 · Business 2/5 · AI/ML about 75% · Track: Bio & Health

  • The problem. India runs about 18 crore malaria tests a year and finds about 2.6 lakh positives, roughly one in 700 (national programme report, Dec 2024; ORF, 14 Oct 2025). The count mixes microscope slides and rapid tests, and the split is not published. A technician who sees one positive in hundreds of slides will miss some. In August 2026 the Health Ministry also asked states to look into unusually high examination rates for duplicate entries (ThePrint/PTI). So the programme has two needs: catch the rare positive, and prove a slide was really read.
  • The idea. A phone clamped to the eyepiece of the lab's existing microscope. It photographs 10–20 fields, marks the objects that look like parasites, and shows them as thumbnails for the technician to confirm or reject. It keeps a dated photo record of every slide. The technician still makes the diagnosis.
  • Who pays. The state malaria programme, through National Health Mission funds. Indian malaria alone is worth perhaps Rs 10–15 crore a year (the designer's estimate, on assumed slide counts). That is too small for a company, so it is a starting point. The same rig could read ordinary blood smears for small private labs, or serve African malaria programmes.
  • The AI/ML core.
    • Stage one: a small object detector (YOLO11n) finds white cells and parasite candidates. Stage two: a small classifier (EfficientNet-B0) throws out look-alikes such as platelets and stain debris.
    • Data is free from the US National Library of Medicine: thick smears from 150 falciparum patients (1,819 phone photos, 84,961 marked parasites), 150 vivax patients and 50 uninfected patients (dataset page).
    • Published result on phone photos: 82.7% sensitivity and 98.4% specificity per image patch on 30 held-out patients (Yang et al.). The slides were from Bangladesh. No India-validated figure exists.
    • Why a human must confirm: at one positive in 700, even a 2% false-alarm rate per slide gives about 14 false alarms for every true case.
    • It cannot tell the two species apart on thick smears, find very faint infections, or read a stain or microscope it has not seen.
  • Why malaria first. TB smear reading is being replaced by molecular tests under national policy. Cervical-cancer slides have no open Indian supply. Anaemia typing is already done by analysers.
  • Who has tried. SigTuple sells bench analysers for blood-cell counts, with no malaria claim (BioSpectrum, 24 Feb 2026). Morphle sells slide scanners and raised $5 million in November 2025 (Entrepreneur India). Turocrates AI, from WTFund's latest cohort, describes itself only as an "OS for precision diagnostics". None sells a phone-on-microscope malaria checker to a public programme.
  • What you could show by noon. Train on the public images, splitting by patient so no patient is in both training and test. The number: sensitivity and specificity per patient on 30 infected and 20 uninfected held-out patients, with the uncertainty range stated (it will be wide). If the biology lab has a microscope with a 100x oil lens, add a second number: false alarms on your own phone photos of known-negative slides. That shows honestly how much accuracy drops on a new microscope.
  • Parts and cost. Borrowed microscope and phone, a 3D-printed clamp, slides and stain. Under Rs 500.
  • The hard question. "Your model learned from Bangladeshi slides. Why would it work in Bastar?" It will not without retraining. The plan starts with a 2,000-slide re-read study in one district.
  • Suits. Shilpy Kochhar (a diagnostic with a BIRAC grant route). Arshia Mal (WTFund backed a pathology-slide device). Karthik Varada (a health outcome).

M4 · Acoustic leak finder for water pipes (survives only in a smaller form)

Buildable overnight 4/5 · Business 2/5 · AI/ML about 50% · Track: Frontier Hardware & Climate

  • The problem. Indian city water utilities lose an average of 38% of treated water before it is billed (ORF citing NIUA, 24 Aug 2025). Leaks are found by crews with listening rods, or by digging.
  • Funded analogue. Puralink, a Sydney company in Peak XV's Surge programme, builds robots that travel inside pipes (Forbes Australia). In India, Solinas Integrity does similar work and raised $5.5 million in August 2026 (Inc42).
  • The idea. Skip the robot. A "listening stick" (a piezo disc or accelerometer on a rod) is touched to a valve or hydrant for ten seconds. A phone records the sound, a model labels it leak, tap use, pump or traffic, and each point lands on a map.
  • The AI/ML core. Convert each clip to a spectrogram, pass it through a pretrained audio model (YAMNet or PANNs) and train a small classifier on top. A free dataset exists: 280 thirty-second recordings from a 47-metre PVC test rig with four leak types (Aghashahi and others, Data in Brief, 2023). Published field accuracy is over 99% on metal pipes and about 89% on plastic (snippet).
  • Why the city-wide version fails in India.
    • Supply is intermittent. Mumbai averaged 5.4 hours of water a day in 2024 (Praja Foundation, snippet). An empty pipe makes no leak noise.
    • During supply hours, household suction pumps on the main add noise. No published method handles that.
    • In plastic pipe the sound dies within metres, so a survey would need a listening point every 20–50 metres.
  • What survives. A survey tool for metal trunk mains and for private networks: campuses, townships, industrial estates. These pay faster than municipalities too.
  • What you could show by noon. Three to four metres of PVC pipe on a lab tap, with a 1.5 mm drilled hole and a screw plug. Record with the plug in and out, at four distances, with and without a small pump running. The number: leak-or-not accuracy on about 120 clips, split by recording session, shown against distance. Compare with a simple loudness threshold. Say plainly that this is a lab rig.
  • Parts and cost. Pipe, fittings, a USB sound card, piezo discs, a small pump. About Rs 1,300.
  • The hard question. "Your pipes are empty most of the day and noisy the rest. How does this work at all?" Only in the supply window, and only once the model has heard Indian pump noise, which nobody has recorded yet.
  • Suits. Karthik Varada (a resource outcome in litres). Vibhore Sharma (a sensor feeding a model).

M2 · Hinglish clinic scribe (did not survive; a smaller piece does)

Buildable overnight 4/5 · Business 2/5 · AI/ML about 80% · Track: AI & Systems

  • Where it started. Freed, backed by Sequoia, writes the clinical note from a recording of the visit and serves over 17,000 clinicians (Sequoia, 5 Mar 2025). The Indian case looked strong: one study of government speciality clinics in Patna found about two minutes per patient and over 200 patients a day (IJCMPH; one hospital, not a national figure).
  • Why it failed. Eka Care already does it. EkaScribe writes prescriptions as well as notes, works in Hindi and many other languages, has a free tier, and costs about $20–25 a month (snippet). Eka is also building an offline version with NVIDIA (Digital Health News). Round 1's claim that solo clinics were an open gap did not hold.
  • What survives. The error that matters in a scribe is a wrong drug or dose on a printed prescription, and no vendor publishes that figure for mixed Hindi-English speech. A drug-and-dose checker would match what was heard against a list of Indian brand names and force the doctor to confirm anything uncertain. It could be sold as a component to scribe makers.
  • What you could show by noon. Record 30 staged consultations using 60 real brand names, half with clinic noise playing. The number: the share of prescription lines where drug, strength, dose, frequency and duration are all correct, with and without the brand-name matcher. Eka has published a test set of medical speech (3,619 English and 320 Hindi clips) that can serve as a second check.
  • The hard question. "Eka has a free tier. Why you?" On the full scribe, no reason. Only the measured drug-line accuracy is new.
  • Suits. Shilpy Kochhar (health delivery). Vibhore Sharma (Capital 2B is an investor in the voice-AI company Ringg).

4. Proptech ideas

Round 1 found ten Indian property problems with public proof. Five went through design and fact-checking. One finding runs through all five: in Indian property, the AI part is usually a feature inside a service that people already pay for, and seldom a software company on its own. The cards say which is which.

P1 · Finding untaxed and unauthorised construction from imagery

Buildable overnight 4/5 · Business 3/5 · AI/ML about 60% · Track: AI & Systems or Space Tech

  • The problem. Property tax is a city's largest income of its own, but tax registers go years without revision. In one Nagpur zone, an AI survey flagged 7,000 of 9,000 properties as different from the register. Inspectors checked 380 at random and found 227 wrong: 12 new buildings and 215 major alterations. The city's arrears exceed Rs 900 crore (Nagpur Today, 29 Oct 2025). Hyderabad's survey found over one lakh defaulters (PropNewsTime), and Delhi began a drone survey of two wards in September 2026 (ThePrint).
  • The idea. One change detector, two buyers. The tax wing wants a list of properties ranked by rupees owed. The enforcement wing wants a list of changes with no building permission on file.
  • Who pays. The municipal corporation. In 2023 Delhi capped a drone and door-to-door survey of about 30,000 properties at Rs 3 crore, roughly Rs 1,000 a property (Prithvi Estates, 15 Mar 2023). Each such survey is stale within two years. The product is a yearly change alert at perhaps Rs 10–20 a property. With 100 large cities and 3 crore properties (assumptions), the ceiling is about Rs 60 crore a year.
  • How it works.
    1. Take two images of the same area from different dates.
    2. Detect where building footprints appeared or grew.
    3. Match each change to a line in the tax register.
    4. Estimate the tax gap: extra built area times the city's rate.
    5. Give inspectors a ranked visit list. Their verdicts become training labels.
  • The AI/ML core.
    • Change-detection models (BIT or ChangeFormer in the open Open-CD toolkit), pretrained on LEVIR-CD, a set of 637 image pairs. They score about 90% on that set, which is American suburbs; expect less on Indian rooftops until fine-tuned.
    • Google's Open Buildings gives free building outlines for India, and a yearly presence layer for 2016–2023 (Open Buildings Temporal).
    • Matching a change to a register entry with a free-text address is often the larger source of error.
  • What imagery can see. This is the key design fact.
    • Free Sentinel-2 images (10 metres per pixel) cannot see a single house.
    • India's Cartosat-3 (0.28 metres) costs Rs 3,860–4,810 for a 17 by 17 km scene and is free to government bodies (NRSC/NSIL price note, Apr 2024). It shows new buildings, sheds and extensions.
    • A new floor is nearly invisible from above. It shows only as a slightly longer shadow. Floors need a drone.
  • Who has tried. Bhubaneswar runs a system built by Vassar Labs on commercial imagery (snippet). The Defence Estates office built its own with BARC and in 2022 reported 1,133 unauthorised changes detected (DGDE, snippet). Other cities hire one-off survey agencies. Nobody was found selling a recurring alert across many cities.

Build a mock register of 150 properties with 30 deliberately out of date. The number: of the top 50 properties the system flags, how many are real changes, judged by two teammates separately. Compare with the free Google yearly layer and with random order.

P4 · Phone scan of a new flat: true carpet area and a snag list

Buildable overnight 5/5 · Business 3/5 · AI/ML about 40% · Track: AI & Systems

  • The problem. Buyers find seepage, hollow tiles and less floor area than they paid for only after taking possession. MahaRERA alone had 6,366 complaints pending in December 2025, of all kinds (PropNewsTime, citing the state Economic Survey). About 5.2 lakh homes were completed in the top seven cities in 2025 (ANAROCK, snippet). The money is concrete: on a 700 sq ft flat at Rs 12,000 a sq ft, a 1% shortfall is Rs 84,000.
  • Funded analogue. Automax.ai (Y Combinator, Fall 2025) sends an inspector to scan a house with a phone's depth sensor, drafts the valuation with AI, and has a licensed appraiser sign (YC launch page).
  • What changes for India. India has no reliable database of comparable sale prices, so AI valuation is dropped. The daily Indian dispute is carpet area, which is a measurement problem. Like Automax, this works best as an inspection service that uses software, not as software alone.
  • How it works.
    1. Scan each room with an iPhone Pro; Apple's free RoomPlan tool returns walls and openings.
    2. Compute carpet area as the RERA Act defines it (section 2(k)): usable floor area including internal partition walls, excluding outer walls, shafts, balconies and terraces.
    3. Compare with the sale agreement and state the shortfall in rupees.
    4. Add snags: cracks from photos, hollow tiles from the sound of a tap, floor slope from the phone's motion sensor.
    5. Produce a report the buyer can send to the builder or to RERA.
  • The AI/ML core. Geometry carries the area; ML does the smaller jobs. A segmentation model finds cracks, an audio classifier separates hollow from solid tiles, and a vision-language model writes each snag line.
  • The accuracy question nobody has answered. No published study gives RoomPlan's floor-area error. On a 3 by 4 metre room, a 5 cm error on each wall is about 3% of the area, which is the whole 3% variation that Maharashtra's model sale agreement allows before a refund is due (an advocate's reading in the Free Press Journal, 29 Jun 2026, not a ruling). So the scan settles a dispute only if each wall is right to 1–2 cm. Where it is not, the app must ask for a laser-meter reading.
  • Which phones. Only iPhone 12 Pro and later Pro models, and iPad Pro, have the sensor. Without one, use a laser distance meter and an in-app sketch.
  • Who has tried. PropCheck says it has done over 45,000 inspections, using laser meters and thermal scanning (propcheck.in; another part of the same page says 10,000-plus). Nemmadi and HomeInspeKtor are similar. All are people-led.
  • Who pays. The buyer, Rs 4,000–6,000 at handover. If 20% of completed homes were inspected (assumption), that is about Rs 52 crore a year of service revenue in the top seven cities. Software licensed to inspection firms would earn about Rs 3 crore.
  • What you could show by noon. The number: average error in room floor area, phone scan against laser meter, over 10 rooms scanned three times each by different people. Compare with one person using a tape. This number does not exist in print, so measuring it is a real result.
  • Parts and cost. A borrowed iPhone Pro, a laser distance meter (Rs 1,500–4,000, estimate), eight tiles and a sheet of plywood for the tap test.
  • The hard question. "PropCheck has done 45,000 inspections. What do you add?" One technician in 90 minutes instead of two engineers in four hours, with a stated accuracy band. They could adopt the same tool.
  • Suits. A revenue-minded mentor (a buyer who pays at a moment of need). Anuj Mehta.

P2 · Move-in and move-out condition report for rented flats

Buildable overnight 4/5 · Business 2/5 · AI/ML about 55% · Track: AI & Systems

  • The problem. Tenants in Bengaluru and Mumbai pay deposits of many months' rent and argue over deductions when they leave. One widely shared case: Rs 1 lakh paid, Rs 19,604 returned (Business Today, 14 Jul 2026). An estimate attributed to NoBroker puts deposits held in six metros at Rs 1.26 lakh crore (RentOk summary); the method is not published, so treat it as a rough size. Neither side has a neutral record of the flat on day one.
  • Verdict up front: a feature. RentOk already sells photo checklists for move-in and move-out. Abroad, several products produce AI condition reports (Property Inspect, Paraspot and others). What is new is the automatic comparison with a priced, itemised deduction.
  • How it works.
    1. At move-in, the app guides a fixed set of photos per room. Both sides confirm.
    2. At move-out, the app shows the old photo as a faint overlay so the new one matches.
    3. Software aligns each pair and finds what changed.
    4. A model labels each change as damage, normal wear, or no real change (a shadow, a moved chair).
    5. A written rule table, not the model, decides what is chargeable, and a rate card prices it.
  • The AI/ML core. Feature matching (LightGlue) aligns the pair; image differencing finds changes; a vision-language model labels them. No published accuracy exists for telling damage from wear indoors.
  • Who pays. Co-living and PG operators, who handle hundreds of move-outs a month and control the app. A licence at Rs 75 a move-out might earn about Rs 6 crore a year (assumptions). It becomes a company only as the evidence layer of a deposit-free renting product.
  • What you could show by noon. Ten hostel rooms. One teammate photographs them. A second stages 30 reversible changes (washable marks, a chalk crack, a missing switch cover) and 30 distractions (lights, curtains, a moved chair). A third re-photographs. The number: real changes caught, and false alarms per room. Compare with handing the model the two photos without alignment.
  • Parts and cost. Phones, tape, chalk, washable markers. Under Rs 300.
  • The hard question. "Why would a landlord agree to a neutral report?" Many will not. That is why the buyer is the operator, who can make it the process.

5. Funded abroad, buildable for India

5.1 What the money is going to

Round 1 read Y Combinator's batches from Winter 2025 on, and deals by a16z, Sequoia Capital and Peak XV since January 2025. It kept the ones that apply AI to an old industry. Three patterns stand out.

  1. AI that does back-office work in old industries. Insurance claims, clinic paperwork, construction estimating, product compliance, truck dispatch. This is the largest group and the closest to what you asked for.
  2. Physical AI. Robots for factories, and the data to train them. Mostly too large for a student team, but the data-collection side already runs through India.
  3. Hardware for energy and defence. Motors, batteries, drones. Not buildable overnight.

Y Combinator also publishes what it wants to fund. Its Winter 2027 list includes "AI for City Hall" (permits, inspections, records), a consent layer for government benefits, and AI that lets non-specialists do finance, legal and medical-billing work (ycombinator.com/rfs). Each has an obvious Indian form. The first is tested in section 4.6 and found already taken.

5.2 Why the Indian version is rarely a copy

The same five differences came up in almost every case.

  • Price. The Indian buyer pays a twentieth of the American price, or less. A scribe that costs hundreds of dollars a month in the US sells for about $20 here, with a free tier.
  • Interface. WhatsApp and a phone call, not a web dashboard joined to other software.
  • Documents. Scanned, handwritten, and in two or more languages.
  • The state is already there. For building plans, land records and health-scheme claims, a government portal or programme already does part of the job.
  • Service beats software. Buyers pay for an outcome: a filed registration, an inspection, a title report. The Indian winner is often a service firm that uses AI to work faster.

One more lesson from the fact-check. Round 1 said "no Indian competitor found" for seven of these ideas. A second pass searched by name. It found direct competitors for four (claim auditing, plan checking, home inspection, the clinic scribe), adjacent ones for two (product compliance, condition reports) and none for one (the trucking voice agent). Treat any "nobody does this in India" claim, including the ones left in this report, as unproven until you have searched by name.

F1 · Product-compliance checker for importers and small sellers

Buildable overnight 5/5 · Business 3/5 · AI/ML about 50% · Track: AI & Systems

  • The problem. A small importer or online seller must know whether a product needs a BIS certificate, what the pack label must say, and whether packaging or battery waste rules apply. The rules move. In August 2025, 187 quality-control orders covered more than 769 products, up from 14 orders in 2014 (Swarajya, 30 Aug 2025). By January 2026 the government had withdrawn 69 orders (ThePrint, snippet). Registering one electronics product costs roughly Rs 75,000 to Rs 1.3 lakh all-in (consultants' own pages, snippet).
  • Funded analogue. Complir does this for European retailers and raised $11 million in September 2026.
  • How it works.
    1. The seller pastes a product listing or photographs the pack.
    2. The system works out what the product is.
    3. It searches a dated rule base and returns the obligations that apply, each with the notification it comes from and whether that rule is in force, deferred or withdrawn.
    4. For a pack photo, it reads the label and lists the mandatory declarations that are missing.
    5. Anything uncertain goes to a human.
  • The AI/ML core. Retrieval over the rule texts (the open bge-m3 embedding model handles Hindi and English), then a language model that must quote the clause it relies on. No quote, no claim. No public benchmark exists for sorting products into Indian regulations.
  • Why India needs its own version. Each regulator publishes separately, by gazette PDF, sometimes in Hindi first. Rules are withdrawn as well as added, so tracking status matters more than in Europe. Keeping the rule base current, with a daily crawl and a named human reviewer, is the product and the main cost.
  • Who has tried. Consultancies do the filings and give the first advice free. BPR Hub ($2.6 million seed, January 2025, snippet) and Zango are adjacent. None was confirmed to cover BIS, labelling and waste rules together.
  • Who pays. Importers of electronics, toys and small appliances, whose real cost is a shipment held at customs. A checker alone is about a Rs 20 crore market (assumptions). The larger business is a compliance firm that uses the AI to file faster and earns the Rs 10–15 thousand filing fee.
  • What you could show by noon. Buy 20 packaged products from the campus store. The number: across 20 packs and 8 required declarations (160 checks), how often the checker correctly finds a missing one. Two teammates read the packs as the truth. Add a second test: 60 products, half in regulated categories, scored against a hand-made answer key.
  • Parts and cost. Software only, plus about Rs 500 of goods.
  • The hard question. "Consultants give this advice free. Why pay you?" For one product, nobody will. They pay for speed across hundreds of products and for an alert when a rule changes.
  • Suits. A revenue-minded mentor (the payer and the price already exist). Anuj Mehta.

F2 · Health-insurance claim checker for small hospitals

Buildable overnight 4/5 · Business 3/5 · AI/ML about 75% · Track: AI & Systems or Bio & Health

  • The problem. Indian insurers handled 3.26 crore health claims in 2024–25 and rejected 8% (Insurance Business, citing IRDAI's annual report). BCG estimates Rs 8,000–10,000 crore a year is lost to fraud, waste and abuse (BCG, 24 Nov 2025). Since March 2021 the regulator has required a rejection to state specific reasons and point to the policy conditions (IRDAI circular, as reported).

  • Which side to build for. The payer side is crowded in India. Consint.AI raised Rs 22 crore in August 2026 (Inc42); Vitraya, Superclaims and insurers' own systems do the same job.     1. The hospital's insurance desk uploads the policy, the discharge summary and the bill.
    2. The system extracts the claim into a structured form and checks the arithmetic.
    3. Coded rules compute the predictable deductions: room-rent caps, co-pay, sub-limits, waiting periods.
    4. For everything else, it finds the relevant policy wording and quotes it.
    5. Before the claim is sent, the desk sees likely deductions and missing documents. After a query comes back, the system drafts the reply.

Compare with giving the same model the whole policy and no rules or quote check.
  • Parts and cost. Software only.
  • The hard question. "Insurers already run AI on claims. Won't theirs always win?" This does not fight the insurer's system. It makes the hospital's file complete before it is sent.
  • Suits. Shilpy Kochhar (health delivery). A revenue-minded mentor.

F4 · Drawing-to-quote for small machine shops

Buildable overnight 4/5 · Business 3/5 · AI/ML about 60% · Track: AI & Systems

  • The problem. A small machine shop gets an enquiry as a photo of a drawing on WhatsApp. The owner reads the dimensions and tolerances, estimates machine time in his head and replies with a price, often a day later. No count of such shops could be found; the problem rests on how the trade works, not on a statistic.
  • Funded analogue. Pre6, in Peak XV's Surge 11 and founded by a co-founder of Udaan, reads drawings and costs parts for large suppliers. It is Indian. The gap is the 10–50 person shop.
  • How it works.
    1. Straighten the photo and find the regions: views, dimensions, tolerance frames, title block, notes.
    2. Read each region separately into a list of features.
    3. The owner checks every value on screen, each boxed on the image.
    4. Rules turn features into operations and machine time; the shop's own hourly rate turns time into a price.
    5. Send the quote on WhatsApp.
  • The AI/ML core.
    • Reading whole drawings with a general model fails. In one study a small fine-tuned model beat GPT-4o and Claude by 52% on this task (arXiv 2411.03707). A detect-then-read pipeline reached 97% with about 5% invented values (arXiv 2505.01530).
    • Five per cent invented values is fatal on a price, so the human check is not optional.
    • Costing starts as rules. It then learns each shop's own multiplier from 30–50 of its past quotes.
  • Why India needs its own version. Photos, not CAD files. Rupees and the owner's language. About Rs 2,000 a month, not enterprise pricing.
  • Who pays. The shop owner. With 60,000 shops (assumption) and 5% adoption, about Rs 7 crore a year.
  • What you could show by noon. Ten drawings, photographed at an angle and also as clean scans. The number: the share of about 150 dimension call-outs read exactly right, phone photo against clean scan. Compare with reading the whole sheet at once. The industrial estates at Kundli and Rai are next to Ashoka; one shop's real quote for three parts would make the demo.
  • Parts and cost. Software only.
  • The hard question. "Isn't costing the hard part, not reading?" Yes. Rules alone are rough; the per-shop learning from past quotes is what tightens it.
  • Suits. Vibhore Sharma above all: his one public quote is about manufacturing that is "slow, expensive, and hard to scale", and Capital 2B led a manufacturing company's seed in 2025.

F3 · Hindi voice agent for a small fleet's check calls

Buildable overnight 4/5 · Business 2/5 · AI/ML about 70% · Track: AI & Systems

  • The problem. About three-quarters of Indian truck operators own fewer than five trucks (Redseer). A transporter using hired trucks learns where a load is, and why it is late, by phoning the driver. Public proof that this call is a real pain is weak.
  • Funded analogue. Hemut (Y Combinator, Spring 2025) makes these calls for American trucking offices.
  • How it works. The agent calls the driver in Hindi and asks three things: where are you, when will you arrive, any problem. It turns the answers into a row on the trip sheet and alerts the office only on exceptions.
  • The AI/ML core. Speech recognition on phone-quality audio (AI4Bharat's open models or Whisper), a small scripted dialogue, and a language model that fills the fields. The interesting piece is place names: drivers say "near the dhaba after Dharuhera", and the system must turn that into a point on the route using a map list and the last toll crossed.
  • What tracking leaves open. Toll-tag data gives the last toll crossed, not live position, and never the reason for a delay. The agent adds status and reasons.
  • Who pays. A transporter with 10–100 trucks, not the small owner. A hosted voice stack costs about Rs 5.5 a minute before phone-line charges (Bolna pricing), so six calls a trip costs more than the Rs 15 a trip one could charge. It works only if calls happen on exceptions.

  • The hard question. "Ringg or Bolna can ship a trucking template next month. Where is your edge?" It is thin: the place-name resolver and the toll-triggered calling.
  • Overlap with your own work. The same technique as your factory-floor voice idea: speech into structured records.

7. Method and limits

7.1 How this was done

  • Round 1, five research agents. Two on the nine mentors and their institutions, one on Indian proptech, two on startups funded since January 2025 by Y Combinator, a16z, Sequoia Capital and Peak XV. 163 web searches.
  • Triage. Fourteen ideas were shortlisted: four read from the mentors' records, five proptech, five Indian versions of funded startups.
  • Round 2, four agents. Two re-opened the sources behind the shortlist and tried to break each claim. Two designed each idea in depth: the system, the model and data, the Indian differences, the business arithmetic and an overnight test. 130 web searches.
  • Writing. The report was written from the notes after applying every correction from round 2.

7.3 What could not be checked

  • The mentors' own words. Only Vibhore Sharma has a verified quote from 2024–26. Section 2's reading of the others rests on what their funds hold and on their careers.
  • The PedalStart mentor. No page confirms the name on the poster or his role.
  • SPARK Night itself. No challenge statements, judges, criteria or details of the Singapore programme are public. Ask the organisers on the day.
  • Pages that would not open. YourStory, LinkedIn, Instagram, X, Reddit and several others blocked the tools. Facts from them are marked "(snippet)": they were seen only in a search summary.
  • Market sizes. Almost every market figure in the idea cards uses at least one assumed count (shops, hospitals, cities). They are marked "(assumption)" and are there so you can change the inputs, not as findings.
  • Model accuracy in India. For most ideas no published Indian accuracy figure exists. That is why each card names one number to measure overnight.
  • Things recalled, not looked up. Dataset sizes for the bare-board PCB sets, the list of phones with a depth sensor, and the wording of two RERA sections came from the design agents' memory and are flagged in their notes.
