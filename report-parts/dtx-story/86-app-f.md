## Appendix F. Strategy and the options in detail

This appendix holds the full detail behind chapter 3. F1 to F9 test the home blood-pressure step loop as a strategy partner and an early-stage deep-tech investor would; F10 to F13 set out every option the team weighed, the scores, the five concept cards and one option set aside late; F14 applies the venture course's frameworks to the chosen concept. Claims-check corrections (D3-01 to D3-03) are applied. A fact whose full description lives in another appendix appears here as one short clause with its ID.

### F1. The question tree

The governing question: "Can a medicine-adjusting loop that starts with blood pressure become a business that improves outcomes for people far from a specialist?" Six sub-questions follow, each graded strong, partial or missing (OUR VIEW).

| Sub-question | What the research supports | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Does the loop work? | Abroad, yes, in randomised trials. In the US (HyperLink), pharmacists adjusting doses from home readings got 57.2% controlled against 30.0% under usual care [D1-01-F04]. In Lesotho, lay workers adjusting one pill with an app got 58% controlled against 48% with referral to a clinic [D3-05-F24]. India's nearest trial was null: in mWellcare, a decision-support app at the visit, against enhanced usual care, left systolic pressure (the upper number) 0.98 mmHg apart, not significant [D1-01-F46]. No Indian randomised trial of the home loop was found (D1-01). Trial details: Appendix B. | Strong abroad; missing in India |
| 2. Is the gap real in India? | Yes. At 24 government-programme clinics 51% of 21,895 registered patients returned; among them control rose from 26.3% to 59.8% (no comparison group) [D1-03-F10]. The cause is less clear: of 157 lapsed patients phoned in Bathinda (not a random sample), 24.8% felt no illness and 22.2% named distance [D3-05-F07]. | Strong on size; partial on cause |
| 3. Can it be delivered safely and legally? | Safety: in rural China, dose-stepping by village doctors under physician supervision brought no more falls, low pressure or kidney harm than in comparison villages [D1-01-F38]. Law: CDSCO (India's device regulator) lists hypertension platforms as medical device software (July 2026 guidance) [D2-04-F07]; the risk class and the pilot permit are questions for counsel and CDSCO [D2-04-F06, D2-04-F14]. The 2020 telemedicine guidelines say only a registered doctor may prescribe and must deliver the prescription directly [D2-04-F17]. No order letting a nurse change a dose was found [D2-04-F18]. | Partial |
| 4. Will anyone pay? | No Indian payer was found paying for remote monitoring or digital therapy as a line item [D2-04-F47]. Our ₹149 a month is an ASSUMPTION (DT3). | Missing |
| 5. Can it be defended? | Not yet. The protocol is public [D1-06-F14]. The government programme (over 2.5 million patients by April 2022) runs on the open-source Simple app, whose protocol model has no step ladder [D2-01-F12, D1-06-F40]. | Missing |
| 6. Can this team start it? | The prototype, yes: the protocol and open readings exist [D1-06-F14, D2-03-F61]. A pilot needs a supervising doctor, an ethics committee and prior registration (search summary only) [D2-04-F45]; the team has none yet. | Partial |

### F4. Where the money is today

This follows the rupee along the chain and names the pool a loop could draw on.

- **Medicines.** The largest pool: about ₹16,371 crore of blood-pressure medicines sold through chemists (Pharmarack, year to December 2025) [D2-05-F01]. One private-brand combination pill a day costs about ₹3,906 a year (OUR ARITHMETIC: ₹10.7 x 365, 2019-20 prices) [D2-05-F17].
- **Devices.** Small and one-time: a cuff costs about ₹2,600 (2026 listings, search summary only) [D1-06-F31].
- **Consultations.** No checked figure in the notes.
- **Programmes.** Diabetes only, loss-making: Sugar.fit earned ₹66.5 crore and lost ₹42 crore in the year to March 2025 [D2-05-F19]. Blood-pressure programme revenue: no figure.

**Which pool (OUR VIEW).** A loop can draw on what a clinic or hospital earns by keeping a patient, or on a medicine maker's patient-support budget (size NOT FOUND). On our model (DT3: OUR ARITHMETIC, ₹149 a month ASSUMPTION) the reachable market is ₹749 crore, under 5% of the pill pool (749 ÷ 16,371), and year-5 revenue is ₹17.9 crore. US$100 million a year needs 52.7 lakh paying patients against 41.9 lakh reachable: the test fails in India alone. The model, counted two ways, is in Appendix E.

### F9. Three questions a partner would press

Each has the honest answer and what would settle it.

1. **"Is this a dose product or a follow-up product?"** On clinic data, mostly follow-up: among programme patients who kept attending, later steps added 6-7 points of control to the first pill's 70-76% (OUR ARITHMETIC: 77 - 70; 82 - 76) [D1-06-F04]. Home data may show more: of 1,578 treated attenders analysed in 12 states (corrected on 14 November from 1,920, the number enrolled) [D4-03-F40], 21.8% looked controlled in clinic but not at home [D3-04-F09]. Settled by: a pilot that counts patients brought back separately from doses changed.
2. **"Who pays, and does that buyer serve the patient the challenge names?"** Nobody yet [D2-04-F47]. ₹1,788 a year (₹149 a month) adds about 46% to one private-brand pill's ₹3,906 (OUR ARITHMETIC: 1,788 ÷ 3,906) [D2-05-F17]; the rural patient sits in the public tier, assumed at ₹15 a month (DT3). Settled by: two clinics signing at a stated price; one state naming the result it would pay for.
3. **"What stops Simple, a hospital or a medicine maker copying it?"** Nothing today [D1-06-F40]. Later: outcome data, a licence, clinic contracts. Settled by: whether logged step responses improve decisions, which takes months of patients.

### F10. The option tree

The options are grouped by what the feedback loop changes; seven were scored (A to G, see F11). The rest were weighed and left out, and each reason given is OUR VIEW.

- **1. A drug dose or a protocol step**
  - **A. Blood pressure**, from a home cuff. Scored 4.35. Card 1.
  - **B. Insulin dose in type 2 diabetes**, from fasting sugar. Scored 3.35. Card 2.
  - **G. Heart-failure medicines**, from weight, pressure, pulse and lab values. Scored 3.10. Card 4.
  - **C. Breathing plan for asthma and COPD (long-term lung disease)**, from an inhaler sensor, peak flow (how hard a person can blow out) and air quality. Scored 3.05. Card 5.
  - **F. Warfarin (a blood thinner)**, from a home INR test (a blood test of clotting time). Scored 2.90. In a pooled review (28 trials, 8,950 people), the trials where patients tested at home and set their own dose showed fewer clots (risk ratio 0.47; below 1 means fewer) and fewer deaths (0.55) than usual care [D1-02-F05]. Self-dosing and self-testing were not compared head to head.
  - **E. Weight-loss injection dose**, from weight and side effects. Scored 2.70. No outcome evidence for digital dose support was found; an independent US review is due in 2027 [D1-01-F41].
  - **Gout medicine**, from blood urate. Not scored: the one trial found had nurses stepping the dose, with no device or software [D1-02-F06].
- **2. Food and behaviour**
  - **Meals guided by a glucose sensor.** Not scored: Indian programmes already sell this with a doctor and a coach [D1-04-F04, D1-04-F06, D1-04-F07].
  - **Lifestyle coaching app for blood pressure.** Not scored: it changes no medicine. Japan has insured one, CureApp HT, since 1 September 2022 [D1-01-F57].
  - **Activity nudges.** Not scored: in a 6-week trial of 44 US adults, the effect on steps faded [D1-02-F31, D2-03-F02].
- **3. Exercise as the dose**
  - **D. Rehabilitation at home**, guided by camera and pulse sensor. Scored 3.35. Card 3.
  - **Handgrip exercise dosed by a load-cell device.** Not scored: checked late in the night and set aside (F13).
- **4. Therapy delivered by the device itself**
  - **Closed-loop insulin pumps.** Not scored: costly hardware. One maker, Insulet, reported US$842.0 million of revenue in the third quarter of 2026 [D1-05-F41].
  - **Wearable nerve stimulation for migraine.** Not scored: the patient triggers it and no sensor closes the loop [D1-02-F12].
  - **Auto-adjusting breathing machines.** NOT RESEARCHED.
- **5. Biofeedback**
  - **Device-guided slow breathing for blood pressure.** Not scored: across 8 trials (494 people, up to 9 weeks) the effect vanished once the 5 trials tied to the maker were left out [D1-02-F11].
  - **Heart-rhythm feedback.** Not scored: a pooled result (18 studies, 1,352 people) showed no fall in stress [D1-02-F15].

#### The three to weigh

OUR VIEW.

**A. Home blood-pressure step loop (recommended, 4.35).**
- *What it is.* Card 1 in F12; the protocol file itself is in Appendix G.
- *Why.* The strongest proof of any option, including deaths and strokes avoided at scale by non-physicians. The largest patient pool: 35.5% of Indian adults aged 20 and over have high blood pressure [D1-03-F01]. The exact Indian protocol text is public. No Indian service was found.
- *Honest test results.* Every branch of two published protocols passes. A simulated cohort built on the programme's own control-by-step figures from 159,292 patients [D1-06-F04], labelled as a simulation. Reading-quality tests on teammates. The four results are set out in Appendix G.
- *Main risk.* Who pays, which is the open business question. And it can look like "just rules", and the mWellcare result will be raised. The answer: the rules are what the trials show works; nobody runs them at home; the hard parts are trusting a home reading, checking the pills were taken before raising a dose, and proving safety.

**B. Insulin dose guidance for type 2 diabetes (3.35).** Good trials and pharma interest. But tonight's test would be thin: no open type 2 simulator exists [D1-06-F30], and the open data covers 3-14 days [D1-06-F11]. Low blood sugar is a real hazard. Sanofi already has a dosing app, cleared in the US (card 2) [D2-04-F24]. Best shown as the second protocol on the same engine, tested on paper.

**D. Adaptive home rehabilitation (3.35).** The easiest demo. But software rehabilitation only matches in-person care and does not beat usual care [D1-02-F07, D1-02-F54], and Lupin says it already holds an Indian Class C device licence for cardiac rehabilitation [D1-04-F15].

#### The other four

OUR VIEW.

- **G. Heart-failure dose loop (3.10).** Strong outcome proof and the best fit to "far from a specialist" [D1-01-F31]. It needs lab values, so it cannot be tested honestly tonight; it is the later protocol for the same engine.
- **C. Breathing plan (3.05).** The most hardware and the best open data [D1-06-F50], but the dose change that works in asthma needs no sensor [D1-02-F37], and sensor inhalers failed commercially.
- **F. Warfarin (2.90).** The best proof of all, a small market, no meter tonight.
- **E. Weight-loss injection support (2.70).** A hot market since generic semaglutide arrived on 20-21 March 2026 [D1-04-F10], with no outcome evidence yet and three Indian players already in it [D1-04-F26, D1-04-F06, D1-04-F27].

### F14. The course frameworks, applied to the home blood-pressure step loop

#### S.M.A.R.T problem statement

To stop blood pressure staying high because the dose change waits for a visit that half the patients never make [R], doctors in private clinics and their patients on blood-pressure medicine [S] need the 30-day review done from home, as a doctor-approved next step of the published protocol [A], shown in one clinic over a 90-day pilot [T], measured by the share of patients under 140/90 and bought at ₹149 a patient a month (ASSUMPTION) [M].

- **S · Specific:** one buyer (a hypothesis) and one gap: the dose step between visits.
- **M · Measurable:** the share under 140/90 at 90 days among patients who would not have returned. The price is an ASSUMPTION.
- **A · Action-oriented:** each cycle ends in one action: step up, support, order tests, refer or hold.
- **R · Relevant:** 51% returned, and control rose among those who did, with no comparison group (F1) [D1-03-F10].
- **T · Time-bound:** a prototype by noon on 14 November 2026; a pilot endpoint at 90 days.

#### The belief we turn upside down

- **Belief we turn over:** a medicine dose can change only when the patient is in front of the doctor.
- **Instead:** the reading travels to the doctor and the decision travels home. The 30-day review happens whether or not the patient returns.

Why we think this solves the problem (OUR VIEW):

- The step that fails in India is the return visit, not the protocol [D1-03-F10].
- Where the dose changed without a clinic visit, pressure fell [D1-01-F02, D1-01-F04].
- Readings alone do not do it: in pooled trials, self-monitoring alone gave 1.0 mmHg against usual care, no effect [D1-01-F05].
- The limit: prompting a clinician during a visit added nothing in mWellcare [D1-01-F46], so the value must come between visits.

### F15. So what for us

OUR VIEW. The judgements from F1 to F14, each said once.

- **The evidence.** The medicine is settled; payment and defence are not. The clinical logic is strong abroad, untested in India, with no proven payer. State the proof as "a protocol inside a funded programme", never as "the protocol alone".
- **Where to start.** The proof is strongest in option groups 1 and 4. Group 4 needs expensive hardware and group 1 needs almost none, so a student team, and a low-resource country, should start in group 1. After the claims check no score moves: A leads by a full point.
- **Why blood pressure first.** Only card 1 has a complete published Indian protocol, a cheap valid home signal, outcome proof at scale and an honest test tonight. Cards 2 and 4 are the same loop with a new protocol file; cards 3 and 5 are different products. Build card 1 tonight, show insulin as a second protocol file on paper, and name heart failure as the later step.
- **The buyer.** The first payer is an urban private clinic, not the rural patient the challenge names. A doctor paid per visit may see a home loop as lost visits, so sell kept patients: return and control at 90 days, not the engine. Staying in care is likely to matter more than the dose step. None of the four things the business must be best at is the rules engine.
- **The weakest leg is the customer.** No interviews, a hypothetical persona, and return-visit figures from public clinics for an assumed private buyer. Do the ten-question interviews next week. The concept passes on evidence and timing; it does not yet pass on customer proof or on defence.
- **The market.** State it plainly: SAM ₹749 crore, SOM ₹17.9 crore in year 5, and a US$100 million test that fails in India alone (OUR ARITHMETIC, DT3). The story needs more protocols, a public tier or other countries.
- **The payer decides whether this is a company or a feature.** Entry is easy and buyers are few. No answer to "who pays" in 90 days means a public-good tool, not a company.
- **Defence.** There is no moat today. Start the slow assets now: a clinical partner, a 90-day pilot, the regulator's class opinion and the record of step responses.
- **Funding.** A prototype alone is not fundable; a signed pilot customer is the next proof. Spend 90 days getting a doctor, a class answer and a paying clinic. Without them, this is a free add-on to the public programme.
- **Discipline.** Agree the stop lines before the pilot. Hold the line on claims: a doctor approves every change, the engine is rules, and nothing built tonight shows an effect on patients.
