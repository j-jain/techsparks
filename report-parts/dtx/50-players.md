## F1. The business view: who is in this business

### What this part shows

This part maps who works in chronic-disease care in India and abroad, what each sells, and how deep each goes on the adaptive depth scale (0 = uses no patient data, 1 = shows data, 2 = reminders, 3 = personalised coaching, 4 = changes the therapy itself). It then shows who failed, why, and where nobody was found.

**What the diabetes programmes sell.** A glucose sensor or meter plus a doctor and a coach. None of their pages says glucose data changes a drug dose by algorithm, so on what is published they sit at depth 3 [D1-04-F02, D1-04-F04, D1-04-F06, D1-04-F07]. Their outcome figures are their own averages with no comparison group (THEIR CLAIM) [D1-04-F06, D1-04-F07]. In practice they sell food and activity coaching around a sensor.

**What the government's tools do and do not do.** IHCI runs a fixed state drug-and-dose protocol. The Simple app keeps a registry, flags overdue patients and sends reminders [D1-04-F19]. Its protocol model stores a drug list and follow-up days, and no step logic was found in it [D1-06-F40]. So the nurse records the reading, but the step-up decision still waits for a visit. 2025-26 figures: NOT FOUND [D1-04-F19].

**What medicine makers do around weight-loss injections.** Generic semaglutide launched on 20-21 Mar 2026, as key patents expired; ₹1,290 a month is one maker's (Natco's) lowest vial price, not the market price, and other makers started from about ₹750-1,300 a month [D1-04-F10]. Dr Reddy's plans obesity centres, onboarding and titration guidance, with no app named (Feb 2026) [D1-04-F11]. HealthifyRx reported a Novo Nordisk India tie-up (search summary only) [D1-04-F26]; BeatO runs a GLP-1 programme [D1-04-F06]. Tracky is the first Indian product found that names dose titration as its purpose [D1-04-F27]. Abroad, Omada leaves prescribing to partner clinicians [D1-05-F14].

### Why companies died

- **Pear** (addiction and insomnia apps): that Medicare did not pay and half of revenue came from three payers could not be confirmed; bankrupt April 2023; assets sold for just over $6M against $130M of liabilities [D1-05-F20, D1-02-F42]. Its products had randomised trials [D1-02-F17]. A panel said it tried to change care and payment at once [D1-05-F21].
- **Akili** (ADHD game): $523,000 of prescription revenue in 2023, net loss $59.5M; sale agreed for about $34M in May 2024 [D1-02-F44]. Staff cuts conflict: 40% (search summary only) [D1-05-F22], or a third in Jan 2023 and 46% in Apr 2024 [D1-02-F44].
- **Better Therapeutics** (diabetes app): trial HbA1c 0.39 points lower than a control app, 669 patients, 90 days [D1-02-F21]; expected no revenue in 2023 (revenue not reported), deficit $134.3M, weighed winding down in Mar 2024 [D1-02-F46].
- **Teva Digihaler**: US distribution and app support stopped 1 June 2024; the US notice gave no reason and a UK recall cited "commercial reasons" [D1-02-F41]; its trial's interval crossed 1 [D1-02-F02].
- **Propeller**: bought by ResMed for $225M in 2019. Sources conflict: still a ResMed company in 2023 [D1-05-F63]; an undated ResMed page says it "has ceased operations" [D1-02-F40].
- **Proteus** (ingestible sensor): bankrupt June 2020, reason NOT FOUND [D1-05-F56].
- **Babylon**: lost NHS contracts; $221M loss on about $1B revenue; UK business sold for just over $600,000 in 2023 [D1-05-F54].
- **Livongo**: bought by Teladoc for $18.5B in 2020 [D1-05-F7]; Teladoc booked a $13.4B goodwill write-down for the whole company in 2022 after its share price fell; its annual report does not name Livongo for the charge [D1-05-F53].
- **Onduo**: Sanofi said it "over-invested" (2019); Verily began phasing it out in 2024 [D1-05-F57].

Common causes:
- No one paid for the app alone [D1-05-F20, D1-02-F44, D1-05-F23].
- Too few prescribers [D1-05-F21].
- Trials that payers did not accept as outcome proof [D1-05-F21].
- Sensors with no therapy change [D1-02-F41, D1-02-F02].
- Valuations ahead of revenue, then no cash [D1-05-F55, D1-04-F23].
- Drop-off: the median of 93 mental-health apps kept 3.3% of users at 30 days [D1-02-F26].

### The gap

OUR VIEW. No Indian service was found that turns home BP or glucose readings into a medicine change between visits [D1-04-F09, D1-04-F19]. Indian programmes stop at coaching or reminders [D1-04-F02, D1-06-F40]. Abroad the space is held by US programmes paid by US payers [D1-05-F13, D1-05-F46]; the low-income-country programmes found are protocol care, not device-linked dose loops [D1-05-F65]. The closest abroad: Closed Loop Medicine (UK) has been in members' voluntary liquidation since 28 Apr 2026 [D3-06-F07], its website now points to a licensing firm [D3-06-F08], and its one trial had no comparison group (205 patients, systolic down 11 mmHg) [D3-06-F09]; Cadence's protocol-bound titration software entered a US regulator's (FDA) pilot on 13 Aug 2026 and is not cleared (THEIR CLAIM) [D3-06-F31]; and Omron, the Japanese cuff maker, has a UK product that drafts a medication plan for the clinician to accept or modify (search summary only) [D3-06-F35]. These are one night's search results: a hospital, drug maker or state may run something unpublished.

### So what for us

OUR VIEW.
- **Stand where nobody was found:** a home BP loop that proposes the next protocol step for a doctor to approve, the only kind of programme PHTI passed [D1-05-F2].
- **Sell to a payer, not a patient.** Pear, Akili and Better Therapeutics had trials yet failed for lack of payment [D1-05-F20, D1-02-F44, D1-02-F46].
- **Build the prescriber in.** Clinician referral adds 40 days of retention [D1-02-F27]; Pear lacked prescribers [D1-05-F21].
- **Never sell the sensor or the reminder as the product,** and keep spending in line with revenue [D1-02-F41, D1-04-F23].
- **Measure what payers count from day one:** share under 140/90, and how many lost patients return [D1-05-F31].
