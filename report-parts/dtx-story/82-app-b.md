## Appendix B. The clinical evidence in detail

This appendix holds the detail behind chapter 2.1 (what actually improves outcomes) and chapter 2.2 (how care has been scaled without specialists). B1 to B10 cover what works, with every trial's design, size, place, comparator, length, confidence interval (CI) and limits, including the null results. B11 to B17 cover how care has been scaled without specialists, in India and abroad. B18 sets out what a doctor would ask. The burden figures and the Indian cohorts are in Appendix A.

### B1. The loop: sense, decide, confirm, act, learn

A therapy loop has five stages. This section says what happens at each and where existing products stop (picture in chapter 1: monitoring stops at the reading; a therapy loop changes the treatment).

- **Sense.** A home device gives a number, valid only if taken correctly. Level 1 products stop here (Appendix A2).
- **Decide.** Software applies a written protocol to this patient's readings and proposes the next step. Coaching apps decide advice, not doses [D1-04-F04]; the protocol model of the government's Simple app stores a drug list and follow-up days, and no step ladder was found in it [D1-06-F40].
- **Confirm.** A doctor, nurse or health worker approves each change; unsafe cases go to a specialist. Trials used pharmacists [D1-01-F04] or village doctors [D1-01-F09]; no Indian service doing this from home readings was found [D1-04-F09].
- **Act.** The patient gets the new dose, medicine or exercise level. Heart-failure telemonitoring trials without a mandated dose protocol found no benefit (B6).
- **Learn.** The next readings show whether it worked, and the loop runs again. At 24 government-programme sites, 10,621 of 21,895 patients did not return [D1-03-F10].

### B2. Five rules the evidence gives

Across six disease areas, a home reading improved outcomes when it led to a changed treatment, and mostly did not when it was only displayed or turned into a reminder. OUR VIEW: five rules follow from that, each with its headline numbers. The full trials are in B4 to B9 and B14.

**1. The reading must change the treatment.** In 15 pooled trials, self-monitoring alone lowered systolic pressure 1.0 mmHg against usual care, no effect; self-monitoring with intensive support (dose changes by doctors, pharmacists or patients, plus education or lifestyle counselling), not dose changes alone, lowered it 6.1 (B4) [D1-01-F05]. Two US heart-failure trials of remote monitoring without a mandated dose protocol found no benefit [D1-01-F32, D1-01-F33], while fast dose stepping after discharge cut readmission or death (B6) [D1-01-F27]. Against usual care, warfarin patients who set their own dose from home tests had fewer deaths; in separate trials, those who left the dose to the clinic did not; the two approaches were not compared head to head (B7) [D1-02-F05].

**2. Who is allowed to decide matters more than the electronics.** In a UK trial, sending readings electronically added a non-significant 1.2 mmHg over paper once the family doctor adjusted doses (B4) [D1-01-F03]. In rural China, supervised village doctors stepping drugs by protocol got 57.0% under 130/80, against 19.9% with enhanced usual care, and cardiovascular events were lower at 4 years, though the programme also gave free or discounted drugs and paid incentives (B14) [D1-01-F09, D1-01-F38]. The Indian null result belongs here: mWellcare gave nurses and doctors an app that prompted treatment steps at the visit, and found no benefit over enhanced usual care. What that null result does and does not show is in B14 [D1-01-F46].

**3. Adherence technology alone does not move outcomes.** Smart pillboxes or medication labels for tuberculosis did not reduce poor outcomes against standard care, and inhaler reminders raised children's adherence with no difference in school absence (B9) [D1-02-F59, D1-02-F01]. OUR VIEW: raising a dose that is not being taken is unsafe, so adherence is an input to the dose decision ("is the current dose being taken before we raise it"), not a product.

**4. Someone must pay for the outcome.** Apps and sensors sold on their own failed. Pear's assets fetched just over $6 million against $130 million of liabilities (2023-2024; B8) [D1-02-F42]; Teva withdrew the Digihaler app and stopped US distribution of its sensor inhalers on 1 June 2024 [D1-02-F41]. Those at scale are paid by employers and insurers for clinician-led programmes (Hinge Health: revenue $234.970 million in the third quarter of 2026 [D1-05-F15, D1-05-F16]) or sell a therapy device (Insulet: $842.0 million [D1-05-F41]). The companies are in Appendix D and the payers in Appendix E.

**5. People stay when their own clinician is in it.** In 8 remote US studies (over 1,00,000 participants) median retention was 5.5 days; clinician referral added 40 days, payment 22 days (one study) [D1-02-F27]. OUR VIEW: a consumer app is the wrong shape. More retention figures are in B9.

### B3. A map of feedback-driven therapies

Five kinds of feedback-driven therapy, sorted by what the loop changes.

### B4. Blood pressure: home readings that change the medicine

Blood pressure has the deepest evidence: primary-care titration trials, a 33,995-person trial of dose stepping by village health workers with four-year hard outcomes, and South Asian and Indian trials, including a null result that shows where a product should not try to add value. This section covers trials where a home reading changed the medicine; dose stepping by non-specialists at scale, and the South Asian and Indian trials, are in B14.

(Two pictures in chapter 2 belong here: the extra fall in systolic pressure against usual care by what was done with the reading, and the share of patients under control with and without a programme that steps the dose. They set separate trials with different patients and end points side by side, so compare the groups, not single bars.)

#### Trials where home readings led to a medicine change

| Trial, year | Country, setting | Patients | What changed, and who decided | Comparator | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TASMINH2, 2010 | UK, 24 general practices | 527, BP above 140/90 despite treatment | Patient self-titrated by a pre-agreed plan, with telemonitoring | Usual care | Systolic 3.7 mmHg lower at 6 months; 5.4 lower at 12 months (95% CI 2.4 to 8.5) [D1-01-F01] |
| TASMIN-SR, 2014 | UK, 59 practices | 552 at high cardiovascular risk (stroke, heart disease, diabetes, kidney disease) | Patient self-titrated by an individual algorithm, home target 120/75 | Usual care | 12-month BP 128.2/73.8 against 137.8/76.3; systolic 9.2 mmHg lower (5.7 to 12.7) [D1-01-F02] |
| TASMINH4, 2018 | UK, 142 practices | 1,182 | GP titrated from home readings, sent on paper or by telemonitoring | Usual care using clinic readings | Clinic systolic -3.5 mmHg (paper) and -4.7 (telemonitoring) at 12 months [D1-01-F03] |
| HyperLink, 2013 | USA, 16 primary care clinics, cluster trial | 450, uncontrolled BP | Pharmacists adjusted drugs from telemonitored home readings for 12 months | Usual care | Controlled 57.2% against 30.0% (at both 6 and 12 months); systolic -10.7 mmHg at 6 months, -9.7 at 12, -6.6 at 18 (six months after the service stopped) [D1-01-F04] |

Limits: TASMINH2 analysed completers only (91%) [D1-01-F01]; TASMIN-SR had primary data from 81% (8.8 mmHg with imputation) [D1-01-F02].

**The pooled analysis.** An analysis of individual patient data from 15 trials (7,138 people, 12 months) compared self-monitoring with usual care [D1-01-F05]:
- Self-monitoring alone: clinic systolic -1.0 mmHg (95% CI -3.3 to 1.2), no effect [D1-01-F05].
- Self-monitoring with intensive support (dose changes by doctors, pharmacists or patients, plus education or lifestyle counselling), not dose changes alone: -6.1 mmHg (95% CI -9.0 to -3.2) [D1-01-F05].
- Self-monitoring worked best in people on fewer drugs and with baseline systolic up to 170 [D1-01-F05].

#### What telemonitoring adds once someone is titrating

Little. In TASMINH4, telemonitoring against paper self-monitoring, with the GP titrating in both arms, gave -1.2 mmHg (95% CI -3.5 to 1.2), not significant [D1-01-F03]. The independent US review by PHTI (Peterson Health Technology Institute, October 2024) rated monitoring-only programmes "not clinically meaningful", and said monitoring "may support adoption for providers who consistently act on monitoring data" [D1-01-F39]. It rated medication-management programmes, where virtual care teams adjust drugs, "clinically meaningful and more rapid", against a threshold of a 5 mmHg systolic fall [D1-01-F39].

#### A lifestyle-only prescription app in Japan

HERB-DH1 tested CureApp HT, an app giving personalised lifestyle coaching with no drug change (adaptive depth 3), in 390 untreated adults with hypertension at 12 sites in Japan, open-label, for 12 weeks [D1-01-F07]. Against lifestyle advice alone: 24-hour ambulatory systolic -2.4 mmHg (95% CI -4.5 to -0.3), home -4.3, office -3.6; no major programme-related safety events to 24 weeks [D1-01-F07]. CureApp HT was approved in April 2022 and covered by national health insurance from 1 September 2022, prescribed by doctors (THEIR CLAIM for "first worldwide") [D1-01-F57]. A price of about 8,000 yen, with the patient paying 30%, appears in a search summary only [D1-01-F57]; the company's June 2026 release gives 7,010 yen for the app as an insured material (THEIR CLAIM) [D2-04-F43].

OUR VIEW: it moved BP about half as much as the titration trials, in patients not yet on drugs; depth 3 helps a little but does not replace dose changes.

#### Home blood pressure in an Indian diabetes trial

A secondary analysis of a company-linked Indian diabetes trial (Twin Health) used home BP to guide drug changes: systolic -7.6 against -3.2 mmHg, in small subgroups [D1-01-F20].

### B5. Diabetes

In type 2 diabetes the same split holds: software that changes the insulin dose works, and monitoring or coaching alone gives small gains. Closed-loop insulin, where the device changes the dose itself every few minutes, is the model case of an adaptive therapy.

#### Insulin-dose software (adaptive depth 4)

- **d-Nav, 2019:** randomised trial, 181 adults on insulin with HbA1c 7.5-11%, 3 US centres, 6 months. A handheld device measured glucose and computed the next dose, plus clinician support, against clinician support alone (both arms 7 contacts). HbA1c fell 1.0% against 0.3% (p<0.0001); low blood sugar 0.29 against 0.29 events a month. Funded by the US NIH; the authors are maker-linked [D1-01-F13].
- **TeleDiab-2, 2019:** randomised, 191 adults starting basal (long-acting) insulin, 4 months. A phone voice system and an app both proposed daily dose changes towards a fasting glucose of about 100 mg/dL. HbA1c -1.44% and -1.48% against -0.92% with standard care. No severe low blood sugar [D1-01-F14]. The country (France) comes from the research agent's knowledge, not the abstract [D1-01-F14].
- **Sanofi My Dose Coach** has observational data only: in the US, 2,517 users, 44% reached their fasting target, low blood sugar in 12-16% of users [D1-01-F15]; in Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, 494 users, 55.9% reached target in a mean 14.8 days, low blood sugar 1.4% [D1-01-F16]. Neither had a control group. Its status in India is unknown (search summary only) [D1-04-F09].

#### Glucose sensors with and without an algorithm

- **Sensor alone (CGM, continuous glucose monitor), not on insulin:** IMMEDIATE, 116 adults, 16 weeks, time in range up 9.9 points, HbA1c 0.3% lower (95% CI 0 to 0.7) [D1-01-F17].
- **Sensor alone, on basal insulin:** MOBILE, 175 adults, 15 US centres, 8 months: HbA1c 0.4% lower (-0.8 to -0.1). Funded by Dexcom, the sensor maker [D1-01-F42]. This is the one exception to "self-monitoring alone has no effect".
- **Sensor plus algorithm:** 2IQP, 2025, 319 adults with type 2 diabetes on insulin, US and Canada, 13 weeks. Automated insulin delivery against own insulin method, with a sensor in both arms. HbA1c 0.6 points lower (-0.8 to -0.4); time in range 14 points higher. Funded by Tandem, the pump maker [D1-01-F43]. Because both arms had a sensor, this isolates what the dose-changing algorithm adds.

#### Closed loop as the model case

In type 1 diabetes, Control-IQ (168 people, 6 months) raised time in range by 11 points against a sensor-augmented pump and ran in closed-loop mode 90% of the time [D1-01-F24]. The iLet bionic pancreas (326 people, 13 weeks) started from body weight alone with no carbohydrate counting and lowered HbA1c by 0.5 [D1-01-F25]. In type 2 diabetes, a 26-person crossover trial with no meal doses gave time in range 66.3% against 32.3% [D1-01-F26]. A 2026 review says the American Diabetes Association now supports automated delivery as the preferred method for some type 2 patients; the ADA text itself was not opened [D1-01-F45].

#### Nutrition and "digital twin" results, with caveats

- **Zeevi 2015, Israel, 800 people:** a model predicted each person's post-meal glucose; HbA1c was not the outcome [D1-01-F22]. **ZOE METHOD** (347 US adults, mostly without diabetes, company-sponsored): triglycerides -0.13 mmol/L [D1-01-F55].
- **Twin Health, India,** 319 people, open-label, 2:1: HbA1c -2.9 against -0.3 with standard care at 1 year, and "72.7% remission"; the remission definition and drug-withdrawal rules are not in the abstract, and author company links were not checked [D1-01-F19].
- **Twin Health, Cleveland Clinic,** 150 people, single site: HbA1c 6.5% or under on no drug except metformin, 71% against 2.4%. Read through a trade article only [D1-01-F56].
- **Virta:** not randomised (262 against 87, self-selected). At 2 years, use of diabetes drugs other than metformin fell from 55.7% to 26.8% [D1-01-F21].

#### The independent verdict

PHTI (March 2024) found US digital tools for type 2 diabetes without a continuous glucose monitor "do not deliver clinically meaningful benefits and result in a higher cost than usual care", with gains "minimal and short-term" [D1-01-F40]. The two exceptions were people with higher HbA1c newly starting insulin, and nutritional ketosis programmes [D1-01-F40]. PHTI's new review of sensor and GLP-1 support reports in 2027 [D1-01-F41].

### B6. Heart failure

Heart failure gives the sharpest contrast in the notes: fast dose stepping cut death or readmission by a third, while several large telemonitoring trials without a mandated dose protocol were null.

#### Fast dose stepping

STRONG-HF (2022) was an open-label trial in 87 hospitals in 14 countries with 1,078 patients admitted with acute heart failure [D1-01-F27]. The high-intensity arm raised drugs to full recommended doses within 2 weeks of discharge, with 4 visits in 2 months checking symptoms, lab tests and NT-proBNP (a blood marker of heart strain). At day 90, full doses: RAS blockers 55% against 2%, beta blockers 49% against 4%. Readmission or death at 180 days: 15.2% against 23.3% (adjusted estimates, not raw shares), RR 0.66 (0.50 to 0.86); the high-intensity arm also reported more side effects, 41% against 29% (B10). The trial stopped early for benefit. Funder: Roche Diagnostics, maker of NT-proBNP tests [D1-01-F27]. Benefit by age: HR 0.51 under 65, 0.73 over 65, no significant difference [D1-01-F28].

A Russian chatbot titration trial (66 patients, 6 weeks) saw 2 against 16 decompensations or deaths but lost 7 of 33 remote patients [D1-01-F51].

#### Telemonitoring: positive and negative trials

| Trial | Patients, place | What happened to the reading | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| TELE-HF, 2010 | 1,653, USA, 180 days | Daily symptoms and weight by phone, reviewed by patients' own clinicians | Readmission or death 52.3% against 51.5%, null [D1-01-F32] |
| BEAT-HF, 2016 | 1,437, California, 180 days | BP, pulse, weight reviewed by central nurses with protocol actions and phone calls; no mandated dose protocol | Readmission aHR 1.03, null; better quality of life [D1-01-F33] |
| GUIDE-HF, 2021 | 1,000, US and Canada | Implanted lung-artery pressure sensor guiding care | HR 0.88 (0.74 to 1.05), neutral overall [D1-01-F34] |
| TIM-HF2, 2018 | 1,571, Germany | Structured remote management | Days lost ratio 0.80 (p=0.046); all-cause death HR 0.70 (0.50 to 0.96) [D1-01-F30] |

OUR VIEW on what separates them: the positive trials had a structured team or explicit dose targets; the null ones sent data to clinicians or nurses without a mandated dose protocol (BEAT-HF's nurses did follow protocol actions). TIM-HF2's benefit grew with distance to a cardiologist, 13% lower risk per doubling of distance [D1-01-F31]. The TELE-HF authors stressed "a thorough, independent evaluation of disease-management strategies before their adoption" [D1-01-F32].

#### Pooled analyses, which conflict

A 2026 meta-analysis of 79 trials (31,669 patients) found remote monitoring cut heart-failure admissions (IRR 0.81) and death (RR 0.90) with no difference by method [D1-01-F53]. Another 2026 meta-analysis of 16 trials (8,618) found a gradient: pressure-guided RR 0.71, structured remote management 0.79, non-invasive telemonitoring alone 0.93, not significant; the authors wrote "passive telemonitoring alone is insufficient" [D1-01-F54]. They used different trial sets [D1-01-F54].

#### The Indian treatment gap

Indian registries show patients discharged on too few guideline drugs, with high death rates, and they report drug classes, not dose levels. The registry table is in Appendix A7 [D1-01-F35, D1-01-F47, D1-01-F48, D1-01-F49, D1-01-F50].

### B7. Other conditions

The other conditions repeat the pattern and add two lessons: a dose loop can work with no sensor at all, and rehabilitation by software matches in-person care without beating usual care.

- **Warfarin.** A Cochrane review of 28 trials (8,950 patients) found that patients who tested INR (a blood-clotting test) at home and adjusted their own dose by a schedule had fewer clots (RR 0.47) and lower death (RR 0.55, 8 trials). Patients who tested at home and left the dose to the clinic had fewer clots (RR 0.69) but no change in death (RR 0.94, 3 trials). Each approach was compared with usual care in separate sets of trials, not with each other. Major bleeding did not change overall (RR 0.95) [D1-02-F05].
- **Gout.** In a UK trial (517 patients, Nottingham, 2 years), nurses titrating urate-lowering drugs to a target reached it in 95% against 30% under GP care, RR 3.18, at £5,066 per quality-adjusted life year. It was not digital: it was a protocol run by nurses [D1-02-F06].
- **Asthma and COPD.** SMART (one inhaler for both maintenance and relief, so each reliever puff also raises the steroid dose) cut exacerbations by RR 0.68 in 16 trials with 22,748 people aged 12 and over [D1-02-F37]. No sensor was needed because the patient's own reliever use is the signal and the inhaler delivers the dose change at that moment. Sensor inhalers did less: reminders raised adherence without changing school absence (B9) [D1-02-F01]; the Digihaler system's odds ratio for control was 1.35, with a credible interval of 0.846 to 2.038 crossing 1, and adherence fell from 79.2% to 68.6% over 6 months [D1-02-F02]; a Propeller trial cut reliever use but not asthma control scores overall (company-affiliated authors) [D1-02-F38]. Commercially, Teva's sensor inhalers were withdrawn (B2, rule 4) [D1-02-F41], and an undated ResMed page says Propeller "has ceased operations" [D1-02-F40].
- **Rehabilitation by software.** Stroke telerehab after discharge (pooled trials, 661 people) was no better than usual care for daily-living activities (SMD 0.00) and about equal to in-person physiotherapy [D1-02-F07]. Lung telerehab, 99% COPD, matched centre rehab on walking distance, with completion 93% against 70% [D1-02-F03]. A Sword Health trial (140 back-pain patients, company-affiliated) found no difference in disability against in-person physiotherapy but lower dropout, 15.7% against 34.3% [D1-02-F08]. PHTI (June 2024) found physiotherapist-guided virtual care comparable to in-person care with lower spending, app-only exercise "unlikely to be effective as total substitutes", and did not separate the effect of adapting exercise to measured performance [D1-02-F54].
- **Biofeedback and stimulation.** Device-guided breathing lowered systolic BP by 3.67 mmHg across 8 trials (494 people), but showed no effect once the 5 manufacturer-linked trials were removed [D1-02-F11]. Nerivio, a sham-controlled migraine stimulator, gave pain relief at 2 hours in 66.7% against 38.8%, but it uses no sensor loop [D1-02-F12].

### B9. Drop-off and adherence technology

People stop using health apps quickly, and the trials show that raising adherence on its own often leaves outcomes unchanged.

#### Retention

- 93 Android mental-health apps with 10,000 or more installs (2019): the median app kept 3.3% of users at day 30 [D1-02-F26].
- Eight remote US studies: median retention 5.5 days, with clinician referral adding 40 days (B2, rule 5) [D1-02-F27].
- Chronic-disease app trials: pooled dropout 43% (40% in randomised trials, 49% in observational studies) [D1-02-F28].
- Engagement strategies helped modestly, SMD 0.33, very low certainty [D1-02-F56].
- With a clinician in the loop, retention was higher: Sword dropout 15.7% [D1-02-F08]; lung telerehab completion 93% [D1-02-F03] (both in B7).

#### Adherence rose; outcomes did not

| Trial | Patients | Adherence result | Outcome result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inhaler reminders, New Zealand, 2015 | 220 children, 6 months | 84% against 30% | School absence 1.9% against 1.7% of days, no difference [D1-02-F01] |
| MedISAFE-BP reminder app, US, 2018 | 411 adults, 12 weeks, against control | Adherence score +0.4 | Systolic -0.5 mmHg, p=0.78, not significant [D1-01-F06] |
| TB pillbox, China, 2015 | 4,173, 36 districts | Missed doses 17.0% against 29.9% (aMR 0.58); text messages no effect | Cure not measured [D1-02-F34] |
| ASCENT TB, four countries, 2025 | 23,483 | Smart pillboxes or medication labels, against standard of care | Poor outcomes not reduced (adjusted ratios 1.13 to 1.49, all intervals crossing 1); two social harms from pillbox disclosure [D1-02-F59] |
| 99DOTS phone-based adherence, 2025 | Interrupted time series, not randomised | 87.2% enrolled | Completion 89.8% against 87.1% before; no change in treatment success [D1-02-F58] |

No trial in the notes changed a tuberculosis treatment from adherence data. In the China trial, the planned step (doctor moves non-adherent patients to intensive management) was "underutilised" [D1-02-F34].

### B11. India's hypertension programme in practice

India and other low-income settings have raised blood-pressure control without specialists by giving routine work to nurses and health workers under a written protocol, backed by free drugs and regular refills. In India the dose change still waits for a doctor's visit, and that is where a home step loop could fit (B17). The India Hypertension Control Initiative (IHCI) shows what a protocol programme does well, and where patients drop out.

#### Design choices

- A protocol of 3 to 4 steps, amlodipine first. Punjab: amlodipine 5 mg, then 10 mg, then add telmisartan 40 mg (to 80 mg), then add chlorthalidone 12.5 mg (to 25 mg). Maharashtra orders the steps differently [D3-05-F01].
- Free drugs, given as 30-day refills at every facility level from health and wellness centres (HWC) to district hospitals; sub-centres are not named [D3-05-F02].
- The open-source Simple app: about 80 seconds to register a patient, 15 seconds for a follow-up, overdue lists with high-risk patients first, one-click nurse calls, automated reminders [D3-05-F03].

#### Who does what

Medical officers start amlodipine and review at 30 days; nurses register patients and record follow-up; Senior Treatment Supervisors support training. Who steps the dose in Maharashtra is not stated [D3-05-F06].

#### Starts and losses

Two cohorts give different loss figures; they differ in sites, years and definition:

| Cohort | Started | Not seen again | Control among those seen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24 sentinel sites, 4 states, registered 2018-19 (detail in Appendix A6) | 21,895 | 49% (51% returned by Jul-Sep 2019) | 26.3% at registration, 59.8% at follow-up [D1-03-F10] |
| Punjab and Maharashtra, started 2018-2021 | 281,209 | 36% (no visit Jan-Mar 2022) | 70% and 76% on amlodipine 5 mg alone; 75% and 81% after step 2 [D3-05-F04] |

District surveys of awareness, treatment and control in nine IHCI districts, 2018-19 against 2023-24, with no control districts, are in Appendix A5 [D1-01-F10, D1-01-F11]. The programme's own deck claims 75 million people with hypertension and/or diabetes on standard care by December 2025 (THEIR CLAIM) [D3-05-F10].

#### Why patients stopped

A Bathinda (Punjab) phone survey of 157 people who had missed visits for over 3 months gives the reasons in numbers; it sits with the other patient voices in Appendix A13 [D3-05-F07].

#### The refill rule

A doctor dispenses the first medicine. Trained paramedical staff dispense the monthly refill only when pressure is controlled; uncontrolled patients are sent to a doctor for a dose change [D3-05-F08].

#### Cost

Amlodipine for the first two steps cut drug cost about 20%, from US$2.50 to US$2.05 per patient per year [D3-05-F05]. A 2022 WHO release gives "as low as Rs 200" per patient per year, a modelled drug cost (SOURCE ESTIMATE) [D3-05-F09]. Neither includes staff; a full cost was not found.

### B12. Who may change a dose in India

The sources found on who may start or change a blood-pressure dose, and how strong each is:

- Opened source: in Bathinda, paramedical staff refill only when pressure is controlled; uncontrolled patients go to a doctor [D3-05-F08].
- Search summary only: state protocol text has the medical officer start and raise the dose; once controlled, the ANM (auxiliary nurse midwife) at the HWC or sub-centre measures pressure and gives a month's medicines [D3-05-F31].
- Search summary only: community health officer (CHO) duties are screening, referral and follow-up; starting treatment sits with the medical officer and staff nurse [D3-05-F30, D3-05-F13].
- NOT FOUND: any order letting a CHO or nurse start or change an antihypertensive dose. The 2023-2030 national NCD guidelines could not be opened.

We cannot say they may, nor that they are barred. It remains a question for state health departments and a lawyer. The legal detail is in Appendix E.

### B13. Tamil Nadu's doorstep scheme

Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam (MTM) brings screening and medicines to the home [D3-05-F15].

- **Scale:** launched August 2021; by August 2024, 1,86,13,872 beneficiaries, with 92,59,821 people with hypertension given medicines (government figures, not audited; THEIR CLAIM) [D3-05-F14].
- **Who delivers:** women health volunteers deliver drugs to registered patients over 45 or with poor mobility (search summary only) [D3-05-F16].
- **Results:** the 2025 survey of 6,856 adults found over 95% of those diagnosed on treatment but a hypertension control rate of 35.4%, denominator not stated (full detail in Appendix A6) [D1-03-F09].
- **It refills; it does not step.** No source says the dose is changed at the door; delivery continues the existing prescription [D3-05-F15].

#### China Rural Hypertension Control Project (CRHCP)

CRHCP was an open cluster trial in 326 villages with 33,995 people aged 40 and over [D1-01-F09]. Village doctors (community health workers) started and stepped drugs by a standard protocol, supervised by primary-care physicians, and coached patients on home BP monitoring. The comparator was enhanced usual care [D1-01-F09].

- **18 months:** BP under 130/80 in 57.0% against 19.9%, a difference of 37.0 points (95% CI 34.9 to 39.1); systolic 14.5 mmHg lower, diastolic 7.1 lower; no treatment-related serious adverse events [D1-01-F09].
- **4 years (median), age 60 and over (22,386 people):** cardiovascular events (heart attack, stroke, heart-failure admission, cardiovascular death) 2.7% against 3.5% a year, HR 0.75 (0.69 to 0.81); all-cause death HR 0.90 (0.83 to 0.98) [D1-01-F38].
- **4 years, under 60 (11,609 people):** cardiovascular events HR 0.64 (0.56 to 0.75); stroke 0.64; heart failure 0.39; cardiovascular death 0.54 [D1-01-F38].
- **Safety at 4 years:** injurious falls, symptomatic low BP, fainting and kidney outcomes did not differ between groups in either age band (B10) [D1-01-F38].

The caveat. The trial registry entry (opened by the claims check) says the intervention also gave free or discounted drugs and paid village doctors incentives tied to control rates [D1-01-F38]. The effect therefore cannot be credited to the protocol alone.

#### COBRA-BPS (Bangladesh, Pakistan, Sri Lanka)

COBRA-BPS was a cluster trial in 30 rural communities in Bangladesh, Pakistan and Sri Lanka with 2,645 adults with hypertension, run for 24 months [D1-01-F37]. Government community health workers made home visits for BP monitoring and counselling; physicians were trained; care was coordinated in the public sector. Systolic fell 9.0 against 3.9 mmHg, a difference of 5.2 (3.2 to 7.1); control under 140/90 was 53.2% against 43.7%, RR 1.22; all-cause death 2.9% against 4.3%, with no confidence interval in the abstract [D1-01-F37]. Health workers monitored and referred; trained physicians changed the drugs [D1-01-F37]. Post-hoc, patients got about 0.11 more drugs each [D1-01-F12].

#### CARRS (India and Pakistan)

In CARRS, a South Asian diabetes trial, non-physician care coordinators plus physician decision-support software doubled the chance of improving HbA1c, BP and LDL cholesterol together, RR 2.1 (1.5 to 2.8) at 12 months [D1-01-F23]. OUR VIEW: the extra ingredient was a person following patients up.

#### mWellcare (India): the decision-support trial that found nothing

mWellcare was an open cluster trial, randomised by centre, in 40 community health centres in India with 3,698 people with hypertension, diabetes or both, run for 12 months [D1-01-F46]. Nurse-managed care with an Android app giving algorithm-based management prompts, a health record and reminders was compared with enhanced usual care. Systolic difference -0.98 mmHg (95% CI -4.64 to 2.67), not significant; HbA1c +0.11 (-0.24 to 0.45). Both arms fell by about 13 mmHg [D1-01-F46]. The authors: "We did not find an incremental benefit of mWellcare over enhanced usual care" [D1-01-F46].

OUR VIEW, what the null result means:
- Prompting a nurse or doctor during a clinic visit added nothing over a well-run protocol programme that already produced a 13 mmHg fall [D1-01-F46].
- The app worked at the visit. It did not act on home readings between visits, and it did not reach patients who never came back.

OUR VIEW, what it does not mean:
- It does not show that between-visit titration fails in India. The notes found no Indian randomised trial of home readings driving a titration protocol (D1-01, NOT FOUND list).
- It does not show that protocols fail. The enhanced usual-care arm improved as much as the app arm.

### B17. Where a home step loop fits

OUR VIEW. This section places the loop against IHCI as it runs today.

**What it adds to IHCI today**
- The dose step for patients who do not return (36-49% [D3-05-F04, D1-03-F10]), who now need a doctor's visit to step up [D3-05-F08]. The loop sends the doctor a proposed step from home readings, approved without a visit.
- A reading trusted enough to act on, so the "controlled or not" rule [D3-05-F08] can run at home.
- It answers distance and missing guidance, not "no sense of being ill" [D3-05-F07].

**What it must borrow:** free 30-day refills [D3-05-F02] or doorstep delivery [D3-05-F14]; a person who follows up [D3-05-F03]; the protocol [D3-05-F01]; a doctor's sign-off [D3-05-F30].

**Closest precedent:** IMPACT-BP, with home cuffs, delivered medicine and remote nurse decisions [D3-05-F27]. Warnings: visit-time prompts added nothing [D1-01-F46], and China's gain came with drugs and incentives [D1-01-F38].

### B18. What a doctor in a small town would ask

OUR VIEW. These are the questions we expect, with what the evidence can and cannot answer.

1. **Time saved.** A primary-care consultation in India lasted about 2 minutes in 2013-2015, and 79.9% of required posts in four specialities in rural community health centres were unfilled in March 2023 (Appendix A9) [D1-03-F24, D1-03-F16]. A product that adds screens to the visit repeats mWellcare (B14) [D1-01-F46]. It must do the reading, the protocol logic and the draft, and leave one approve-or-change action. How long approval takes in practice: NOT RESEARCHED.
2. **Which protocol.** Doctors will trust a protocol they already use. Trials that worked used pre-agreed plans or standard protocols [D1-01-F01, D1-01-F09]; an Indian state protocol with steps, thresholds and 30-day re-checks already exists [D1-06-F14].
3. **Can I overrule it.** In every positive BP trial a named person held the authority: patient by a pre-agreed plan, GP, pharmacist or supervised village doctor [D1-01-F01, D1-01-F03, D1-01-F04, D1-01-F09]. The design must let the doctor change or refuse any proposal, and record why.
4. **Are home readings valid.** Home and clinic targets differ (TASMIN-SR used a home target of 120/75) [D1-01-F02]; the rules for a trustworthy reading are in Appendix C.
5. **Who is liable** if a proposed step harms a patient, and what licence the tool needs. The clinical evidence cannot answer this; see Appendix E.
6. **Who pays me** for the time. Not answerable from the clinical evidence; see Appendix E.

### B20. So what for us

OUR VIEW.
- **Build at adaptive depth 4.** The active ingredient is the treatment change, not the sensor and not the nudge. The reading must come back as a proposed dose change, or we sit with the null trials [D1-01-F05, D1-01-F39]. A sensor earns its place only if it changes a decision that a cheaper signal cannot; warfarin and SMART show the dose rule itself carries the benefit (B7).
- **India lacks decide, confirm and act between visits** (B1).
- **Give the decision to a named person under a written protocol, and make approving it take seconds.** The authority to change the dose between visits, and someone acting on it, is what worked; electronics added little once someone titrated [D1-01-F03, D1-01-F09]. A doctor will judge the product on minutes and liability before outcomes (B18). In India, design for a doctor approving every dose change (B12).
- **Aim at the gap mWellcare left:** between visits, from home readings, among the 49% who do not return [D1-01-F46, D1-03-F10].
- **Software was part of every working programme and the whole of none.** The largest controlled gains came where a non-doctor could step the dose, with free drugs or one pill; monitoring, counselling or prompts gained less or nothing. Each fix in B15 keeps patients on drugs; none changes the dose.
- **IHCI already has the protocol, free drugs, a record and an overdue list.** It lacks a dose decision for the uncontrolled patient who does not go back to the doctor. In Tamil Nadu the drugs already reach the door at the same dose; high treatment with low control is the gap a dose step addresses.
- **Write a safety branch for each known harm** (swelling, low BP, falls, kidney, low sugar) and report it from day one [D1-01-F01, D1-01-F38]. Protocol titration has a good safety record when the protocol has stop rules. The known costs are more minor side effects, leg swelling in self-managed BP care, and low blood sugar with insulin, so each needs its own safety branch.
- **Insulin titration is the second protocol** for the same loop and has trial support, but low blood sugar makes it riskier, and the large nutrition effects come from company-linked or non-randomised studies.
- **Heart failure** fits the challenge's "far from a specialist" best, but the working loop needs lab values and visits, so it belongs on the roadmap, not in tonight's build.
- **Trial success did not make prescription-app companies viable.** Who pays is a business question (Appendix E). A clinician's name on the programme keeps people in it.
- **The three-question test.** The five rules in B2 turn into three questions any idea must pass:
  1. **What changes.** After a home reading the prescription is different (level 4), within days.
  2. **Who decides.** A general doctor, nurse or health worker, under a written protocol; unsafe cases go to a specialist.
  3. **What moves, and who pays.** One endpoint passes an accepted threshold in 90 days (Appendix A3), and a named payer wants it.
