## G. The strategist's view

### G1. What this part shows

This part tests the home blood-pressure step loop as a strategy partner and an early-stage deep-tech investor would: one governing question, the choices, money, forces, risks and the next 90 days. Claims-check corrections (D3-01 to D3-03) are applied; judgements carry OUR VIEW.

### G2. The question tree

The governing question: "Can a medicine-adjusting loop that starts with blood pressure become a business that improves outcomes for people far from a specialist?" Six sub-questions follow, each graded strong, partial or missing.

| Sub-question | What the research supports | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Does the loop work? | Abroad, yes, in randomised trials. US, HyperLink: 450 adults, pharmacists adjusting doses from home readings against usual care, 57.2% against 30.0% controlled at 6 and 12 months [D1-01-F04]. Lesotho: 547 adults, lay workers adjusting one pill with an app against referral to a clinic, 58% against 48% controlled at 12 months [D3-05-F24]. India's nearest trial was null: mWellcare, 3,698 people at 40 health centres, 12 months, a decision-support app at the visit against enhanced usual care, systolic (the upper number) 0.98 mmHg apart, not significant [D1-01-F46]. No Indian randomised trial of the home loop was found (D1-01). | Strong abroad; missing in India |
| 2. Is the gap real in India? | Yes. At 24 government-programme clinics 51% of 21,895 registered patients returned; among them control rose from 26.3% to 59.8% (no comparison group) [D1-03-F10]. The cause is less clear: of 157 lapsed patients phoned in Bathinda (not a random sample), 24.8% felt no illness and 22.2% named distance [D3-05-F07]. | Strong on size; partial on cause |
| 3. Can it be delivered safely and legally? | Safety: in rural China (33,995 people, 4 years) dose-stepping by village doctors under physician supervision brought no more falls, low pressure or kidney harm than in comparison villages [D1-01-F38]. Law: CDSCO (India's device regulator) lists hypertension platforms as medical device software (July 2026 guidance) [D2-04-F07]; the risk class and the pilot permit are questions for counsel and CDSCO [D2-04-F06, D2-04-F14]. The 2020 telemedicine guidelines say only a registered doctor may prescribe and must deliver the prescription directly [D2-04-F17]. No order letting a nurse change a dose was found [D2-04-F18]. | Partial |
| 4. Will anyone pay? | No Indian payer was found paying for remote monitoring or digital therapy as a line item [D2-04-F47]. Our ₹149 a month is an ASSUMPTION (DT3). | Missing |
| 5. Can it be defended? | Not yet. The protocol is public [D1-06-F14]. The government programme (over 2.5 million patients by April 2022) runs on the open-source Simple app, whose protocol model has no step ladder [D2-01-F12, D1-06-F40]. | Missing |
| 6. Can this team start it? | The prototype, yes: the protocol and open readings exist [D1-06-F14, D2-03-F61]. A pilot needs a supervising doctor, an ethics committee and prior registration (search summary only) [D2-04-F45]; the team has none yet. | Partial |

So what for us (OUR VIEW): the medicine is settled; payment and defence are not.

### G4. How to win

The business must beat everyone at four things, each with a measure (OUR VIEW).

| Must be best at | Why it decides the result | "Better" is measured by |
|---|---|---|
| Trusting a home reading from any cuff | An arm hanging at the side adds 6.5 mmHg systolic (133 adults, US) [D2-02-F16] | Share of patients completing a valid 3-day block each cycle [D2-01-F1]; photo-reading error [D2-02-F4] |
| A safe decision in seconds of doctor time | A consultation lasted about 2 minutes (2013-2015) [D1-03-F24] | Median seconds per approval; share decided within 7 days; step-ups proposed for patients who missed doses |
| Keeping patients sending readings | In a Punjab trial of 388 patients, WhatsApp reminders did not raise return visits: 21.8% against 19.6% [D3-04-F50] | Share sending blocks at 3 and 6 months; lapsed patients brought back |
| Proof a payer accepts | Pear had a randomised trial yet won too few payers and prescribers, a trade-press review said [D1-02-F17, D1-05-F21] | Gain in share under 140/90 at 90 days against comparison clinics |

So what for us (OUR VIEW): none of the four is the rules engine.

### G5. Where the money is today

This follows the rupee along the chain and names the pool a loop could draw on.

- **Medicines.** The largest pool: about ₹16,371 crore of blood-pressure medicines sold through chemists (Pharmarack, year to December 2025) [D2-05-F01]. One private-brand combination pill a day costs about ₹3,906 a year (OUR ARITHMETIC: ₹10.7 x 365, 2019-20 prices) [D2-05-F17].
- **Devices.** Small and one-time: a cuff costs about ₹2,600 (2026 listings, search summary only) [D1-06-F31].
- **Consultations.** No checked figure in the notes.
- **Programmes.** Diabetes only, loss-making: Sugar.fit earned ₹66.5 crore and lost ₹42 crore in the year to March 2025 [D2-05-F19]. Blood-pressure programme revenue: no figure.

Which pool (OUR VIEW). A loop can draw on what a clinic or hospital earns by keeping a patient, or on a medicine maker's patient-support budget (size NOT FOUND). On our model (DT3: OUR ARITHMETIC, ₹149 a month ASSUMPTION) the reachable market is ₹749 crore, under 5% of the pill pool (749 ÷ 16,371), and year-5 revenue is ₹17.9 crore. US$100 million a year needs 52.7 lakh paying patients against 41.9 lakh reachable: the test fails in India alone.

So what for us (OUR VIEW): a doctor paid per visit may see a home loop as lost visits. Sell kept patients.

| Future | What it means | Early sign to watch |

| A. | B. | C. No payer, either class | The patient is the only buyer. The loop survives as a free feature of the public programme | Pilot clinics decline to pay after the free period; outpatient insurance stays under 0.1% of outpatient spending (industry report, search summary only) [D2-05-F10] |

So what for us (OUR VIEW): the payer decides whether this is a company or a feature.

### G10. Three questions a partner would press

Each has the honest answer and what would settle it.

1. **"Is this a dose product or a follow-up product?"** On clinic data, mostly follow-up: among programme patients who kept attending, later steps added 6-7 points of control to the first pill's 70-76% (OUR ARITHMETIC: 77 - 70; 82 - 76) [D1-06-F04]. Home data may show more: of 1,920 treated attenders in 12 states, 21.8% looked controlled in clinic but not at home [D3-04-F09]. Settled by: a pilot that counts patients brought back separately from doses changed.
2. **"Who pays, and does that buyer serve the patient the challenge names?"** Nobody yet [D2-04-F47]. ₹1,788 a year (₹149 a month) adds about 46% to one private-brand pill's ₹3,906 (OUR ARITHMETIC: 1,788 ÷ 3,906) [D2-05-F17]; the rural patient sits in the public tier, assumed at ₹15 a month (DT3). Settled by: two clinics signing at a stated price; one state naming the result it would pay for.
3. **"What stops Simple, a hospital or a medicine maker copying it?"** Nothing today [D1-06-F40]. Later: outcome data, a licence, clinic contracts. Settled by: whether logged step responses improve decisions, which takes months of patients.

So what for us (OUR VIEW): no answer to question 2 in 90 days means a public-good tool, not a company.

### G12. So what for us

OUR VIEW.

- **Strong clinical logic abroad, untested in India, no proven payer.**
- **Sell return and control at 90 days, not the engine.** Staying in care is likely to matter more than the dose step.
- **India alone misses US$100 million a year on our model (OUR ARITHMETIC, DT3).** The story needs more protocols, a public tier or other countries.
- **Spend 90 days getting a doctor, a class answer and a paying clinic.** Without them, this is a free add-on to the public programme.
