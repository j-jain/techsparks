## D. The doctor's view

### What this part shows

Across six disease areas, a home reading improved outcomes when it led to a changed treatment, and mostly did not when it was only displayed or turned into a reminder. This part sets out the trials behind that pattern, their safety record, the Indian results (including one important null result), and what a doctor would need before trusting a product built on them.

### Blood pressure

![Share of patients under control with and without a programme that steps the dose](adaptive-care-pictures/21-share-controlled.png)

*Chart: three settings where someone other than a specialist was allowed to step the dose. The China programme also gave free or cheaper medicines and paid for results; the India figures are before and after among the 51% who returned, with no comparison group [D1-01-F04, D1-01-F09, D1-03-F10].*

Blood pressure has the deepest evidence: primary-care titration trials, a 33,995-person trial of dose stepping by village health workers with four-year hard outcomes, and South Asian and Indian trials, including a null result that shows where a product should not try to add value.

#### (a) Trials where home readings led to a medicine change

| Trial, year | Country, setting | Patients | What changed, and who decided | Comparator | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TASMINH2, 2010 | UK, 24 general practices | 527, BP above 140/90 despite treatment | Patient self-titrated by a pre-agreed plan, with telemonitoring | Usual care | Systolic 3.7 mmHg lower at 6 months; 5.4 lower at 12 months (95% CI 2.4 to 8.5) [D1-01-F01] |
| TASMIN-SR, 2014 | UK, 59 practices | 552 at high cardiovascular risk (stroke, heart disease, diabetes, kidney disease) | Patient self-titrated by an individual algorithm, home target 120/75 | Usual care | 12-month BP 128.2/73.8 against 137.8/76.3; systolic 9.2 mmHg lower (5.7 to 12.7) [D1-01-F02] |
| TASMINH4, 2018 | UK, 142 practices | 1,182 | GP titrated from home readings, sent on paper or by telemonitoring | Usual care using clinic readings | Clinic systolic -3.5 mmHg (paper) and -4.7 (telemonitoring) at 12 months [D1-01-F03] |

Limits: TASMINH2 analysed completers only (91%) [D1-01-F01]; TASMIN-SR had primary data from 81% (8.8 mmHg with imputation) [D1-01-F02]. A pooled analysis of individual patient data from 15 trials (7,138 people) found self-monitoring worked best in people on fewer drugs and with baseline systolic up to 170 [D1-01-F05].

#### (b) Dose stepping by non-physicians at scale

The China Rural Hypertension Control Project (CRHCP) was an open cluster trial in 326 villages with 33,995 people aged 40 and over [D1-01-F09]. Village doctors (community health workers) started and stepped drugs by a standard protocol, supervised by primary-care physicians, and coached patients on home BP monitoring. The comparator was enhanced usual care [D1-01-F09].

- **18 months:** BP under 130/80 in 57.0% against 19.9%, a difference of 37.0 points (95% CI 34.9 to 39.1); systolic 14.5 mmHg lower, diastolic 7.1 lower; no treatment-related serious adverse events [D1-01-F09].
- **4 years (median), age 60 and over (22,386 people):** cardiovascular events (heart attack, stroke, heart-failure admission, cardiovascular death) 2.7% against 3.5% a year, HR 0.75 (0.69 to 0.81); all-cause death HR 0.90 (0.83 to 0.98) [D1-01-F38].
- **4 years, under 60 (11,609 people):** cardiovascular events HR 0.64 (0.56 to 0.75); stroke 0.64; heart failure 0.39; cardiovascular death 0.54 [D1-01-F38].
- **Safety at 4 years:** injurious falls, symptomatic low BP, fainting and kidney outcomes did not differ between groups in either age band [D1-01-F38].

The caveat. The trial registry entry (opened by the claims check) says the intervention also gave free or discounted drugs and paid village doctors incentives tied to control rates [D1-01-F38]. The effect therefore cannot be credited to the protocol alone.

#### (c) The Indian evidence

**The national programme's surveys.** The India Hypertension Control Initiative (IHCI) surveyed the same 9 districts in 5 states twice. In 2018-19, of 7,047 people with hypertension, 52.4% were aware, 40.8% treated and 14.5% controlled; in two districts control was under 5% [D1-01-F10]. In 2023-24, of 7,324, 58.5% were aware, 44.7% treated and 21.2% controlled, a gain of 6.7 points in control [D1-01-F11]. This is a before-after survey with no control districts [D1-01-F11]. In the IHCI cohort of 21,895 registered patients, 51% returned for follow-up; among those who returned, control rose from 26.3% to 59.8%, with no comparison group [D1-03-F10].

**The South Asian trial.** COBRA-BPS was a cluster trial in 30 rural communities in Bangladesh, Pakistan and Sri Lanka with 2,645 adults with hypertension, run for 24 months [D1-01-F37]. Government community health workers made home visits for BP monitoring and counselling; physicians were trained; care was coordinated in the public sector. Systolic fell 9.0 against 3.9 mmHg, a difference of 5.2 (3.2 to 7.1); control under 140/90 was 53.2% against 43.7%, RR 1.22; all-cause death 2.9% against 4.3%, with no confidence interval in the abstract [D1-01-F37]. Health workers monitored and referred; trained physicians changed the drugs [D1-01-F37]. Post-hoc, patients got about 0.11 more drugs each [D1-01-F12].

**The decision-support trial that found nothing.** mWellcare was an open cluster trial in 40 community health centres in India with 3,698 people with hypertension, diabetes or both, run for 12 months [D1-01-F46]. Nurse-managed care with an Android app giving algorithm-based management prompts, a health record and reminders was compared with enhanced usual care. Systolic difference -0.98 mmHg (95% CI -4.64 to 2.67); HbA1c +0.11 (-0.24 to 0.45). Both arms fell by about 13 mmHg [D1-01-F46]. The authors: "We did not find an incremental benefit of mWellcare over enhanced usual care" [D1-01-F46].

OUR VIEW, what the null result means:
- Prompting a nurse or doctor during a clinic visit added nothing over a well-run protocol programme that already produced a 13 mmHg fall [D1-01-F46].
- The app worked at the visit. It did not act on home readings between visits, and it did not reach patients who never came back.

OUR VIEW, what it does not mean:
- It does not show that between-visit titration fails in India. The notes found no Indian randomised trial of home readings driving a titration protocol (D1-01, NOT FOUND list).
- It does not show that protocols fail. The enhanced usual-care arm improved as much as the app arm.

Contrast a South Asian diabetes trial, CARRS: non-physician care coordinators plus physician decision-support software doubled the chance of improving HbA1c, BP and LDL cholesterol together, RR 2.1 (1.5 to 2.8) at 12 months [D1-01-F23]. OUR VIEW: the extra ingredient was a person following patients up.

A secondary analysis of a company-linked Indian diabetes trial (Twin Health) used home BP to guide drug changes: systolic -7.6 against -3.2 mmHg, in small subgroups [D1-01-F20].

#### (d) A lifestyle-only prescription app in Japan

HERB-DH1 tested CureApp HT, an app giving personalised lifestyle coaching with no drug change (adaptive depth 3), in 390 untreated adults with hypertension at 12 sites in Japan, open-label, for 12 weeks [D1-01-F07]. Against lifestyle advice alone: 24-hour ambulatory systolic -2.4 mmHg (95% CI -4.5 to -0.3), home -4.3, office -3.6; no major programme-related safety events to 24 weeks [D1-01-F07]. CureApp HT was approved in April 2022 and covered by national health insurance from 1 September 2022, prescribed by doctors (THEIR CLAIM for "first worldwide") [D1-01-F57]. A price of about 8,000 yen, with the patient paying 30%, appears in a search summary only [D1-01-F57]; the company's June 2026 release gives 7,010 yen for the app as an insured material (THEIR CLAIM) [D2-04-F43].

OUR VIEW: it moved BP about half as much as the titration trials, in patients not yet on drugs; depth 3 helps a little but does not replace dose changes.

#### (e) What telemonitoring adds once someone is titrating

Little. In TASMINH4, telemonitoring against paper self-monitoring, with the GP titrating in both arms, gave -1.2 mmHg (95% CI -3.5 to 1.2), not significant [D1-01-F03]. The independent US review by PHTI (Peterson Health Technology Institute, October 2024) rated monitoring-only programmes "not clinically meaningful", and said monitoring "may support adoption for providers who consistently act on monitoring data" [D1-01-F39]. It rated medication-management programmes, where virtual care teams adjust drugs, "clinically meaningful and more rapid", against a threshold of a 5 mmHg systolic fall [D1-01-F39].

**So what for us.** OUR VIEW: the authority to change the dose between visits, and someone acting on it, is what worked. Electronics added little once a clinician was titrating. Our product's value must sit where mWellcare did not look: between visits, from home readings, among the half who do not return.

### Diabetes

In type 2 diabetes the same split holds: software that changes the insulin dose works, and monitoring or coaching alone gives small gains. Closed-loop insulin, where the device changes the dose itself every few minutes, is the model case of an adaptive therapy.

**Insulin-dose software (adaptive depth 4).**
- d-Nav, 2019: randomised trial, 181 adults on insulin with HbA1c 7.5-11%, 3 US centres, 6 months. A handheld device measured glucose and computed the next dose, plus clinician support, against clinician support alone (both arms 7 contacts). HbA1c fell 1.0% against 0.3% (p<0.0001); low blood sugar 0.29 against 0.29 events a month. Funded by the US NIH [D1-01-F13].
- TeleDiab-2, 2019: randomised, 191 adults starting basal (long-acting) insulin, 4 months. A phone voice system and an app both proposed daily dose changes towards a fasting glucose of about 100 mg/dL. HbA1c -1.44% and -1.48% against -0.92%. No severe low blood sugar [D1-01-F14]. The country (France) comes from the research agent's knowledge, not the abstract [D1-01-F14].
- Sanofi My Dose Coach has observational data only: in the US, 2,517 users, 44% reached their fasting target, low blood sugar in 12-16% of users [D1-01-F15]; in Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, 494 users, 55.9% reached target in a mean 14.8 days, low blood sugar 1.4% [D1-01-F16]. Neither had a control group. Its status in India is unknown (search summary only) [D1-04-F09].

**Glucose sensors with and without an algorithm.**
- Sensor alone (CGM, continuous glucose monitor), not on insulin: IMMEDIATE, 116 adults, 16 weeks, time in range up 9.9 points, HbA1c 0.3% lower (95% CI 0 to 0.7) [D1-01-F17].
- Sensor alone, on basal insulin: MOBILE, 175 adults, 15 US centres, 8 months: HbA1c 0.4% lower (-0.8 to -0.1). Funded by Dexcom, the sensor maker [D1-01-F42].
- Sensor plus algorithm: 2IQP, 2025, 319 adults on insulin, US and Canada, 13 weeks. Automated insulin delivery against own insulin method, with a sensor in both arms. HbA1c 0.6 points lower (-0.8 to -0.4); time in range 14 points higher. Funded by Tandem, the pump maker [D1-01-F43]. Because both arms had a sensor, this isolates what the dose-changing algorithm adds.

**Closed loop as the model case.** In type 1 diabetes, Control-IQ (168 people, 6 months) raised time in range by 11 points against a sensor-augmented pump and ran in closed-loop mode 90% of the time [D1-01-F24]. The iLet bionic pancreas (326 people, 13 weeks) started from body weight alone with no carbohydrate counting and lowered HbA1c by 0.5 [D1-01-F25]. In type 2 diabetes, a 26-person crossover trial with no meal doses gave time in range 66.3% against 32.3% [D1-01-F26]. A 2026 review says the American Diabetes Association now supports automated delivery as the preferred method for some type 2 patients; the ADA text itself was not opened [D1-01-F45].

**Nutrition and "digital twin" results, with caveats.**
- Zeevi 2015, Israel, 800 people: a model predicted each person's post-meal glucose; HbA1c was not the outcome [D1-01-F22]. ZOE METHOD (347 US adults, mostly without diabetes, company-sponsored): triglycerides -0.13 mmol/L [D1-01-F55].
- Twin Health, India, 319 people, open-label, 2:1: HbA1c -2.9 against -0.3 at 1 year and "72.7% remission"; the remission definition and drug-withdrawal rules are not in the abstract, and author company links were not checked [D1-01-F19].
- Twin Health, Cleveland Clinic, 150 people, single site: HbA1c 6.5% or under on no drug except metformin, 71% against 2.4%. Read through a trade article only [D1-01-F56].
- Virta: not randomised (262 against 87, self-selected). At 2 years, use of diabetes drugs other than metformin fell from 55.7% to 26.8% [D1-01-F21].

**The independent verdict.** PHTI (March 2024) found US digital tools for type 2 diabetes without a continuous glucose monitor "do not deliver clinically meaningful benefits and result in a higher cost than usual care", with gains "minimal and short-term" [D1-01-F40]. The two exceptions were people with higher HbA1c newly starting insulin, and nutritional ketosis programmes [D1-01-F40]. PHTI's new review of sensor and GLP-1 support reports in 2027 [D1-01-F41].

**So what for us.** OUR VIEW: insulin titration is the second protocol for the same loop and has trial support, but low blood sugar makes it riskier, and the large nutrition effects come from company-linked or non-randomised studies.

### Heart failure

Heart failure gives the sharpest contrast in the notes: fast dose stepping cut death or readmission by a third, while several large telemonitoring trials without a mandated dose protocol were null.

**Fast dose stepping.** STRONG-HF (2022) was an open-label trial in 87 hospitals in 14 countries with 1,078 patients admitted with acute heart failure [D1-01-F27]. The high-intensity arm raised drugs to full recommended doses within 2 weeks of discharge, with 4 visits in 2 months checking symptoms, lab tests and NT-proBNP (a blood marker of heart strain). At day 90, full doses: RAS blockers 55% against 2%, beta blockers 49% against 4%. Readmission or death at 180 days: 15.2% against 23.3% (adjusted estimates, not raw shares), RR 0.66 (0.50 to 0.86); the high-intensity arm also reported more side effects (see Safety). The trial stopped early for benefit. Funder: Roche Diagnostics, maker of NT-proBNP tests [D1-01-F27]. Benefit by age: HR 0.51 under 65, 0.73 over 65, no significant difference [D1-01-F28]. A Russian chatbot titration trial (66 patients, 6 weeks) saw 2 against 16 decompensations or deaths but lost 7 of 33 remote patients [D1-01-F51].

**Telemonitoring: positive and negative trials.**

| Trial | Patients, place | What happened to the reading | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| TELE-HF, 2010 | 1,653, USA | Daily symptoms and weight by phone, reviewed by patients' own clinicians | Readmission or death 52.3% against 51.5%, null [D1-01-F32] |
| BEAT-HF, 2016 | 1,437, California | BP, pulse, weight reviewed by central nurses with protocol actions and phone calls; no mandated dose protocol | Readmission aHR 1.03, null; better quality of life [D1-01-F33] |
| GUIDE-HF, 2021 | 1,000, US and Canada | Implanted lung-artery pressure sensor guiding care | HR 0.88 (0.74 to 1.05), neutral overall [D1-01-F34] |
| TIM-HF2, 2018 | 1,571, Germany | Structured remote management | Days lost ratio 0.80 (p=0.046); death HR 0.70 (0.50 to 0.96) [D1-01-F30] |

OUR VIEW on what separates them: the positive trials had a structured team or explicit dose targets; the null ones sent data to clinicians or nurses without a mandated dose protocol (BEAT-HF's nurses did follow protocol actions). TIM-HF2's benefit grew with distance to a cardiologist, 13% lower risk per doubling of distance [D1-01-F31]. The TELE-HF authors stressed "a thorough, independent evaluation of disease-management strategies before their adoption" [D1-01-F32].

**Pooled analyses, which conflict.** A 2026 meta-analysis of 79 trials (31,669 patients) found remote monitoring cut heart-failure admissions (IRR 0.81) and death (RR 0.90) with no difference by method [D1-01-F53]. Another 2026 meta-analysis of 16 trials (8,618) found a gradient: pressure-guided RR 0.71, structured remote management 0.79, non-invasive telemonitoring alone 0.93, not significant; the authors wrote "passive telemonitoring alone is insufficient" [D1-01-F54]. They used different trial sets [D1-01-F54].

**The Indian treatment gap.** In the Trivandrum registry (1,205 admissions, 16 hospitals, Kerala, 2013), only 25% of patients with weak pumping were discharged on all three drug classes, and 5-year mortality was 59% [D1-01-F35]. The national registry (10,851 patients, 53 hospitals) found 47.5% of eligible patients on guideline drugs, 90-day mortality 14.2% [D1-01-F47] and 1-year 22.1% [D1-01-F48]. A second registry (5,182 patients) found adherence to all three classes of 18.8% in men and 15.9% in women; one registry counts prescriptions, the other adherence [D1-01-F49]. After a heart attack with weak pumping, use of one class (MRA) fell from 69% to 6% within a year [D1-01-F50]. The registries report drug classes, not dose levels [D1-01-F35, D1-01-F47].

**So what for us.** OUR VIEW: heart failure fits the challenge's "far from a specialist" best, but the working loop needs lab values and visits, so it belongs on the roadmap, not in tonight's build.

### Other conditions, briefly

The other conditions repeat the pattern and add two lessons: a dose loop can work with no sensor at all, and rehabilitation by software matches in-person care without beating usual care.

- **Warfarin.** A Cochrane review of 28 trials (8,950 patients) found that patients who tested INR at home and adjusted their own dose by a schedule had fewer clots (RR 0.47) and lower death (RR 0.55). Patients who tested at home and left the dose to the clinic had fewer clots (RR 0.69) but no change in death (RR 0.94). Each approach was compared with usual care in separate sets of trials, not with each other. Major bleeding did not change overall (RR 0.95) [D1-02-F05].
- **Gout.** In a UK trial (517 patients, Nottingham, 2 years), nurses titrating urate-lowering drugs to a target reached it in 95% against 30% under GP care, RR 3.18, at £5,066 per quality-adjusted life year. It was not digital: it was a protocol run by nurses [D1-02-F06].
- **Asthma and COPD.** SMART (one inhaler for both maintenance and relief, so each reliever puff also raises the steroid dose) cut exacerbations by RR 0.68 in 16 trials with 22,748 people aged 12 and over [D1-02-F37]. No sensor was needed because the patient's own reliever use is the signal and the inhaler delivers the dose change at that moment. Sensor inhalers did less: reminders raised adherence without changing school absence [D1-02-F01]; the Digihaler system's benefit interval crossed 1 and adherence fell from 79.2% to 68.6% over 6 months [D1-02-F02]; a Propeller trial cut reliever use but not asthma control scores overall (company-affiliated authors) [D1-02-F38]. Commercially, Teva withdrew the Digihaler app and US inhalers on 1 June 2024 [D1-02-F41], and an undated ResMed page says Propeller "has ceased operations" [D1-02-F40].
- **Rehabilitation by software.** Stroke telerehab after discharge was no better than usual care for daily-living activities (SMD 0.00) and about equal to in-person physiotherapy [D1-02-F07]. Lung telerehab, 99% COPD, matched centre rehab on walking distance, with completion 93% against 70% [D1-02-F03]. A Sword Health trial (140 back-pain patients, company-affiliated) found no difference in disability against in-person physiotherapy but lower dropout, 15.7% against 34.3% [D1-02-F08]. PHTI (June 2024) found physiotherapist-guided virtual care comparable to in-person care with lower spending, app-only exercise "unlikely to be effective as total substitutes", and did not separate the effect of adapting exercise to measured performance [D1-02-F54].
- **Biofeedback and stimulation.** Device-guided breathing lowered systolic BP by 3.67 mmHg across 8 trials, but showed no effect once the 5 manufacturer-linked trials were removed [D1-02-F11]. Nerivio, a sham-controlled migraine stimulator, gave pain relief at 2 hours in 66.7% against 38.8%, but it uses no sensor loop [D1-02-F12].

**So what for us.** OUR VIEW: a sensor earns its place only if it changes a decision that a cheaper signal cannot. Warfarin and SMART show the dose rule itself carries the benefit.

### Drop-off and adherence technology

People stop using health apps quickly, and the trials show that raising adherence on its own often leaves outcomes unchanged.

**Retention.**
- 93 Android mental-health apps with 10,000 or more installs (2019): the median app kept 3.3% of users at day 30 [D1-02-F26].
- Eight remote US studies, over 100,000 people: median retention 5.5 days; referral by a clinician added 40 days, payment 22 days (one study) [D1-02-F27].
- Chronic-disease app trials: pooled dropout 43% (40% in randomised trials, 49% in observational studies) [D1-02-F28].
- Engagement strategies helped modestly, SMD 0.33, very low certainty [D1-02-F56].
- With a clinician in the loop, retention was higher: Sword dropout 15.7% [D1-02-F08]; lung telerehab completion 93% [D1-02-F03].

**Adherence rose; outcomes did not.**

| Trial | Patients | Adherence result | Outcome result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inhaler reminders, New Zealand, 2015 | 220 children, 6 months | 84% against 30% | School absence 1.9% against 1.7%, no difference [D1-02-F01] |
| MedISAFE-BP reminder app, US, 2018 | 411 adults, 12 weeks | Adherence score +0.4 | Systolic -0.5 mmHg, p=0.78 [D1-01-F06] |
| TB pillbox, China, 2015 | 4,173, 36 districts | Missed doses 17.0% against 29.9% (aMR 0.58); text messages no effect | Cure not measured [D1-02-F34] |

### What a doctor in a small town would ask

OUR VIEW. These are the questions we expect, with what the evidence can and cannot answer.

1. **Time saved.** A primary-care consultation in India lasted about 2 minutes in 2013-2015 [D1-03-F24], and 79.9% of required posts in four specialities (surgeon, physician, obstetrician-gynaecologist, paediatrician) in rural community health centres were unfilled in March 2023 [D1-03-F16]. A product that adds screens to the visit repeats mWellcare [D1-01-F46]. It must do the reading, the protocol logic and the draft, and leave one approve-or-change action. How long approval takes in practice: NOT RESEARCHED.
2. **Which protocol.** Doctors will trust a protocol they already use. Trials that worked used pre-agreed plans or standard protocols [D1-01-F01, D1-01-F09]; an Indian state protocol with steps, thresholds and 30-day re-checks already exists [D1-06-F14].
3. **Can I overrule it.** In every positive BP trial a named person held the authority: patient by a pre-agreed plan, GP, pharmacist or supervised village doctor [D1-01-F01, D1-01-F03, D1-01-F04, D1-01-F09]. The design must let the doctor change or refuse any proposal, and record why.
4. **Are home readings valid.** Home and clinic targets differ (TASMIN-SR used a home target of 120/75) [D1-01-F02]; reading rules are in part E.
5. **Who is liable** if a proposed step harms a patient, and what licence the tool needs. The notes in this part cannot answer this; see part F2.
6. **Who pays me** for the time. Not answerable here; see part F2.

**So what for us.** OUR VIEW: a doctor will judge the product on minutes and liability before outcomes.

### So what for us

OUR VIEW.
- Build at adaptive depth 4: the reading must come back as a proposed dose change, or we sit with the null trials [D1-01-F05, D1-01-F39].
- Give the decision to a named person under a written protocol, and make approving it take seconds; electronics added little once someone titrated [D1-01-F03, D1-01-F09].
- Aim at the gap mWellcare left: between visits and among the 49% who do not return [D1-01-F46, D1-03-F10].
- Treat adherence as an input to the dose rule, never as the product [D1-02-F59, D1-01-F06].
- Write a safety branch for each known harm (swelling, low BP, falls, kidney, low sugar) and report it from day one [D1-01-F01, D1-01-F38].
