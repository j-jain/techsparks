## Appendix A. The challenge, the burden and the voices in detail

This appendix holds the detail behind chapter 1: what the fund's challenge asks a product to do (A1 to A3), how many people in India it concerns and how few are controlled (A4 to A8), why care breaks down between visits (A9 to A12), and what patients and doctors say in public (A13). The trials behind the challenge's demands are in Appendix B.

### A1. The challenge, phrase by phrase

This section turns each phrase of the fund's challenge into what a product must do. The middle column is our reading of each phrase. Terms are explained in A15.

| The fund's words | What a product must do (OUR VIEW) | Facts behind it |
|---|---|---|

### A2. What "beyond monitoring and reminders" rules in and out

This document scores every product on an "adaptive depth" scale from 0 to 4. The fund names levels 1 and 2. OUR VIEW: it is asking for level 4.

| Level | Examples in the notes | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|---|
| 0: uses no patient data | Nerivio migraine wearable, switched on by the patient, rated 0-1 [D1-02-F12]. India: none rated 0 | Nothing adapts. |
| 1: shows data | India: pocket ECGs (search summary only) [D1-04-F32]. Abroad: Omron VitalSight [D1-01-F39] | Self-monitoring alone: no effect [D1-01-F05]; one exception, a glucose sensor (Appendix B5) [D1-01-F42]. |
| 2: reminders, generic content | India: government Simple app, with overdue lists and reminder messages [D3-05-F03]. Abroad: Medisafe reminder app | Medisafe reminder app against control: 0.5 mmHg, not significant (Appendix B9) [D1-01-F06]. |
| 3: coaching from the person's own data | India: Sugar.fit, BeatO, Fitterfly [D1-04-F04, D1-04-F06, D1-04-F07]. Abroad: CureApp HT, Japan | CureApp HT against lifestyle advice alone: 24-hour systolic 2.4 mmHg lower (Appendix B4) [D1-01-F07]. |
| 4: changes the therapy (dose, protocol step, exercise prescription) | India: only Sanofi's My Dose Coach found, its Indian results unconfirmed [D1-04-F09]. Abroad: d-Nav; insulin pumps [D1-05-F41] | d-Nav dose device against clinician support alone: HbA1c (three-month average sugar) down 1.0 point against 0.3 (Appendix B5) [D1-01-F13]. |

**The line between 3 and 4.** Level 3 changes the advice: what to eat, how to exercise. Level 4 changes the prescription: a new dose or protocol step. A clinician approving each change keeps it at level 4. Indian diabetes programmes sit at 3: no page says sensor data changes a drug dose [D1-04-F04, D1-04-F06, D1-04-F07]. The test: after the software runs, the prescription is different.

The loop that level 4 needs (sense, decide, confirm, act, learn) is set out in Appendix B1 (picture in chapter 1).

### A6. Treatment is not control, and half do not return

Two Indian data sets show that getting people onto medicine is not enough, and that people who return do far better than those who do not.

**Tamil Nadu survey.** The state's doorstep programme, Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam, was surveyed in 6,856 adults aged 30+ across all 38 districts (paper abstract, 2025; a South First news report gives 6,809) [D1-03-F09]. Screening reached 79.4% for diabetes and 82.6% for hypertension, and over 95% of diagnosed people were on treatment. Control rates remained low: 9.8% for diabetes and 35.4% for hypertension; the abstract does not say whether these are shares of everyone with the disease, the diagnosed or the treated [D1-03-F09]. How the scheme delivers medicines is in Appendix B13.

**IHCI cohort.** A government programme with a fixed drug protocol. The cohort registered 21,895 patients at 24 sentinel sites in Punjab, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra and Telangana (Jan 2018-Jun 2019), followed up Jul-Sep 2019, with no comparison group [D1-03-F10].
- Only 11,274 (51%) returned [D1-03-F10].
- Among returners, control rose from 26.3% to 59.8% [D1-03-F10].
- Control was 70.4% in primary care against 52.2% in secondary care [D1-03-F10].
- Protocol drugs: amlodipine, telmisartan or losartan, chlorthalidone or hydrochlorothiazide; 44.6% were on amlodipine 5 mg alone at follow-up [D1-03-F11].

A second IHCI cohort, in Punjab and Maharashtra, gives a different loss figure; the two are compared in Appendix B11.

**Return visits.** About 27% of registered IHCI patients did not return in Jan-Mar 2021 (search summary only) [D1-03-F13]. IHCI enrolled about 4 million patients, about 12.5% of the estimated total, by around 2022 (search summary only) [D1-03-F13]. Pooled Indian non-adherence to BP drugs is 48%, with a prediction interval of 9-90% (search summary only) [D1-03-F44]. The reasons patients give for missing visits are in A13.

### A7. Heart failure and stroke in India

No national count of heart failure was found, and the estimates differ widely: 1.3 to 4.6 million cases in a 2010 estimate built from disease-specific figures, whose authors say no surveillance exists (quoted in a 2024 review) [D3-07-F24], against about 8 to 10 million, about 1% of the population, in a 2016 review of local and global studies (search summary only) [D3-07-F25]. For stroke, the Global Burden of Disease study put India's prevalent cases at 9.4 million in 2021, up from 4.4 million in 1990, with over 1.25 million new cases a year (press report quoting the authors) [D3-07-F26].

The registries show patients leave hospital on too few of the drugs that work and stop them afterwards. The three guideline classes are beta blocker, RAS blocker (ACE inhibitor or ARB) and MRA.

### A9. The specialist and time gap

Few specialists work where most patients live, and doctors who see chronic patients have very little time per visit. This is what the challenge's phrase "patients who cannot access frequent specialist support" means in India.

**Rural community health centre (CHC) specialist posts, March 2023** [D1-03-F16]:

| Specialty | Shortfall | Share of required posts |
|---|---|---|
| Surgeon | 4,578 | 83.3% [D1-03-F16] |
| Physician | 4,499 | 81.9% [D1-03-F16] |
| Paediatrician | 4,425 | 80.5% [D1-03-F16] |
| Obstetrician-gynaecologist | 4,078 | 74.2% [D1-03-F16] |

The article's rows sum to 17,580, not 17,551 [D1-03-F16]. The shortfall has stayed near 17,500 since 2014-15 [D1-03-F17].

**Specialist counts.** The only figures are from a 2017 parliamentary committee (search summary only): about 4,000 cardiologists, 1,200 nephrologists and 650 endocrinologists, against the committee's estimated needs of 88,000, 40,000 and 28,000 [D1-03-F18]. The government told Parliament it does not hold speciality-wise doctor data (news report of the reply, 13 Apr 2025) [D3-07-F22].

**Consultation length.** Indian primary-care visits lasted about 2.3 minutes (2013, stopwatch, 412 visits) and 2 minutes (2015) [D1-03-F24]. Japan's, for comparison, lasted 10.2 minutes in 2012 (A8) [D1-03-F24].

**Doctor ratio and its caveat.** The government gave 1:811 in Rajya Sabha (about 2 Dec 2025), counting 13,88,185 allopathic doctors plus 7,51,768 AYUSH practitioners (traditional medicine) and "assuming that 80 per cent" are available [D1-03-F40]. Allopathic doctors alone give a lower ratio. OUR ARITHMETIC from the note: 13,88,185 x 0.8 = about 11.1 lakh, about 0.79 per 1,000 people if the population is about 1.4 billion (an assumption, not sourced) [D1-03-F40].

### A10. Cost and follow-up

Families pay a large share of health costs themselves, and the share rose in the latest year on record.

- **Out-of-pocket share:** 39.4% of total health expenditure in 2021-22 and 43.41% in 2022-23 (National Health Accounts released 27 May 2026, via a news report) [D1-03-F23]. In 2022-23 household spending rose by ₹26,375 crore while government spending fell by nearly ₹49,000 crore [D1-03-F23].
- **Per visit:** medicines were 61.15% of spend per outpatient spell in a Delhi report; another report says median outpatient spend is zero (search summary only) [D1-03-F49].
- **Cheaper medicines:** 18,646 Jan Aushadhi Kendras (generic medicine shops), Feb 2026, priced 50-80% below branded [D1-03-F20].
- **Diabetes spend:** about US$109.5 per person and over US$9.8 billion in total (IDF 2024) [D1-03-F28].

The cost of a chronic-care visit to the household, and how long a dose change is delayed, were not found (A14).

### A13. What patients and doctors say in public

The team has done no interviews, so this section stands in until it does. It reports published interview studies, surveys, public app reviews and posts, labelled PUBLISHED STUDY, PUBLIC REVIEW or FORUM POST. None of it is customer evidence gathered by the team; reviews are self-selected and studies were read as abstracts only.

#### Why patients miss follow-up and stop medicines

Eight Indian interview studies (PUBLISHED STUDY) name the same causes: feeling well, cost, family duties, fear of long-term drugs, traditional medicine.

The same reasons appear in numbers in one IHCI district. A Bathinda (Punjab) phone survey of 157 people who had missed visits for over 3 months, a small convenience sample, found: no sense of being ill 24.8%, distance 22.2%, no guidance or reminder 15.2%, acute illness 8.2% [D3-05-F07].

#### Home monitors in Indian homes

Few patients own a home cuff, most taught themselves, and home readings show that many "controlled" patients are not.

- **Who has one.** Of 2,588 clinic-attending people with hypertension and caregivers in North India, 18.1% used a home monitor (urban 20.5%, rural 8.5%) [D3-04-F06]. No household figure exists; a web claim of under 5% cites no source [D3-04-F22].
- **Who taught them.** 75.2% reported "learning HBPM themselves through observation, videos, and reading"; 24.7% were trained by a health worker. Technique was excellent in 15.9% of patients and 5.9% of caregivers [D3-04-F06].
- **Do doctors act on home readings.** About 88% of 832 Indian doctors recommend home monitoring, but estimate only 30.4% of patients measure; 31.3% say to measure before tablets, about 30% give no instructions [D3-04-F07]. Only 15.1% knew the correct home thresholds [D3-04-F18].
- **What home readings reveal.** In GRAND (18 centres, 12 states, 1,920 treated regular attenders enrolled, 1,578 with valid clinic and home readings analysed, 7 days of home readings), 77.7% of those analysed looked controlled by clinic pressure but 55.9% by clinic plus home; 21.8% had masked uncontrolled pressure (high at home, normal in clinic) [D3-04-F09, D4-03-F40]. Corrected on 14 November: the 13 November edition gave these as shares of the 1,920 (Appendix I). Median knowledge of home monitoring was 1 out of 7 [D3-04-F10].

#### What doctors and health workers say

Doctors describe heavy workloads, and many private doctors do not follow the state protocol.

- **Workload.** In Kolar, Karnataka: "Doctors face very high workloads, especially in the public sector"; care is "very doctor-centred" [D3-04-F16]. Meghalaya reports staff vacancies and limited training [D3-04-F12, D3-04-F20].
- **The state protocol.** Of 30 private doctors in Bhopal, 19 did not follow it, citing "fear of either losing patients due to a lack of immediate blood pressure control or causing drug-related adverse effects" and a preference for fixed-dose combinations (two drugs in one tablet) [D3-04-F15].
- **Health workers with a decision tool.** IMPACT Diabetes, a cluster trial in 16 villages in two states (418 people, 9 months): ASHAs (village health workers) with a tablet tool under physician supervision, against usual care. HbA1c fell by 0.5 or more in 21.8% against 10.3%; physician visits 85.0% against 29.8%; "acceptable and feasible for patients, ASHAs, and physicians" [D3-04-F17]. Diabetes, not blood pressure.

No Indian doctor, nurse or health worker was found speaking about dose steps from home readings (A14).

#### Reminders and messages

In a Punjab trial at two public hospitals, 388 patients with uncontrolled pressure got two myth-busting WhatsApp reminders before their visit or none; attendance was 21.8% against 19.6%, a null result, and beliefs did not change [D3-04-F50]. A Delhi study of 110 patients (counselling, voice calls, SMS) reported control rising from 14.7% to 52.9%, with no control group [D3-04-F51]. Who holds the phone that receives such messages is in A12.

### A15. Terms used in this document

- **Adaptive depth:** 0-4 score for how far a product changes treatment (A2).
- **Adherence:** taking the medicine as prescribed.
- **ANM:** auxiliary nurse midwife.
- **ASHA:** a village health worker.
- **AYUSH:** practitioners of traditional medicine.
- **Basal insulin:** long-acting insulin.
- **Biofeedback:** a body signal shown back to the patient.
- **Care cascade:** following people with a condition through four steps: has it, knows it, is treated, is controlled.
- **CGM:** continuous glucose monitor (a glucose sensor).
- **CHC:** community health centre.
- **CHO:** community health officer.
- **Closed loop:** a device that measures and adjusts therapy itself.
- **COPD:** chronic lung disease.
- **Denominator:** the group a percentage is taken of.
- **Digital therapeutic:** software that changes one patient's treatment.
- **DKA (diabetic ketoacidosis):** a dangerous acid build-up.
- **Endpoint:** the result a trial is judged on.
- **Fixed-dose combination:** two drugs in one tablet.
- **Hazard ratio:** a risk ratio measured over time; below 1 means fewer events.
- **HbA1c:** blood test of average sugar over about three months.
- **Heart-failure guideline drug classes:** beta blocker, RAS blocker (ACE inhibitor or ARB) and MRA.
- **HWC:** health and wellness centre.
- **IHCI:** India Hypertension Control Initiative, a government programme with a fixed drug protocol.
- **INR:** clotting test that sets the warfarin dose.
- **Insulin inertia:** delay in starting or raising insulin.
- **Masked uncontrolled pressure:** high at home, normal in clinic.
- **NCD:** non-communicable disease.
- **Not significant:** small enough to be chance (a null result).
- **NT-proBNP:** a blood marker of heart strain.
- **Protocol:** a written step-by-step treatment plan.
- **Randomised trial:** people get treatments by chance.
- **Risk ratio:** risk with treatment divided by risk without; below 1 means lower risk.
- **SMART:** one inhaler for both maintenance and relief, so each reliever puff also raises the steroid dose.
- **Systolic:** the upper blood-pressure number, in mmHg (millimetres of mercury).
- **Time in range:** share of the day with sugar at 70-180 mg/dL.
- **Titration:** stepping a dose towards a target.

### A16. So what for us

OUR VIEW.
- **The fund asks for a routine treatment decision between visits,** from a home reading, by someone the patient can reach. Level 4 on the adaptive depth scale is the entry condition, however good the sensor (A2).
- **Blood pressure first.** On any count it is the largest pool: 35.5% of adults 20+, against 11.4% for diabetes on the same survey [D1-03-F01].
- **The leak is after treatment starts.** Two steps of the cascade leak, diagnosis and control. Over 95% of diagnosed Tamil Nadu patients are treated, yet the hypertension control rate was 35.4% (denominator not stated) [D1-03-F09]. The step from "treated" to "controlled" depends on the dose being raised and the medicine being taken, which is the decision our loop prepares.
- **Screening and enrolment are already huge** and run at crore scale [D1-03-F20]; the missing piece is the dose change between visits. A loop that feeds the existing protocol and records fits the system rather than competing with it.
- **The return visit is the bottleneck.** 51% of IHCI patients returned, and among those who returned control rose from 26.3% to 59.8%, with no comparison group [D1-03-F10]. Our loop must reach the half who do not come back, between visits. For blood pressure, the pilot should measure the share under 140/90 at 90 days among patients who would not have returned.
- **The scarce thing is the prescriber's decision,** which waits for a visit that about half the patients do not make. About 80% of required rural CHC posts in four specialities (surgeon, physician, obstetrician-gynaecologist, paediatrician) were unfilled in March 2023 [D1-03-F16], and visits lasted about two minutes in 2013-2015 [D1-03-F24]. A two-minute visit has no time to read two weeks of home readings. Software should prepare the next protocol step for a general doctor or nurse to approve, with the specialist owning the protocol.
- **Patient costs.** Avoided trips save patients money, but without a rupee figure per visit we cannot yet claim a saving.
- **Not only an Indian product.** Treated but not controlled is the gap in Japan too.
- **Heart failure.** A clear dose gap with high death rates, but safe titration needs lab values, so heart failure is a later protocol for the same engine.
- **Assume most patients do not own a cuff.** Only 18.1% used one even among clinic attenders [D3-04-F06]. The loop must bring the cuff, the teaching and the thresholds.
- **Feeling well** recurs as a reason to stop, and a home reading is one thing that can contradict it.
- **A doctor's approval cannot be assumed.** A doctor may not trust the state protocol, so each proposal must show why the step is safe, answering the fears of slow control and side effects [D3-04-F15].
- **Never rely on a reminder alone.** WhatsApp reminders did not raise attendance [D3-04-F50]. A message only works if it reaches the person who holds the phone and leads to a decision.
- **Design for the household phone.** 57.4% of rural women own a phone [D1-03-F42], so about four in ten do not (OUR ARITHMETIC: 100 - 57.4 = 42.6%). The design must work on a shared phone, through a health worker, and by voice, in the local language [D3-04-F05, D3-04-F23].
- **Risk: home readings bring bad news.** 21.8% of treated clinic attenders had masked uncontrolled pressure [D3-04-F09]; expect fear of more drugs and the urge to stop [D3-04-F02].
