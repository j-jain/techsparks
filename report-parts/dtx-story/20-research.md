## 2. What the research found

Chapter 1 showed a large, treatable population, a system that loses half of it between visits, and a market that stops before the dose. This chapter condenses what the research found, in plain words. It starts with the clinical evidence on what actually improves outcomes, then turns to how others have scaled care without specialists, what the technology can and cannot do, who is already doing this, where the money is and what the rules allow.

### 2.1 What actually improves outcomes

> **In short.**
> - Outcomes improved when a home reading led to a changed treatment, and mostly did not when the reading was only shown or turned into a reminder.
> - Who was allowed to change the dose mattered more than the electronics.
> - Raising adherence on its own did not move outcomes.
> - The same pattern holds in diabetes, heart failure, warfarin and asthma, and the known harms of dose stepping can be met with stop rules.

The trials sort into five rules. OUR VIEW: the sorting is ours; the numbers are from the notes.

**Rule 1. The reading must change the treatment.** Across 15 pooled trials (7,138 people, 12 months), self-monitoring of blood pressure alone lowered systolic pressure (the upper number, in mmHg, millimetres of mercury) by 1.0 mmHg against usual care: no effect. With intensive support (dose changes by doctors, pharmacists or patients, plus education or counselling), not dose changes alone, it fell 6.1 mmHg [D1-01-F05]. Where the dose itself moved, the falls were larger. In the UK trial TASMIN-SR (552 high-risk patients, randomised, meaning people got each treatment by chance), patients stepping their own dose by an agreed plan had systolic pressure 9.2 mmHg lower than under usual care at 12 months [D1-01-F02]. In the US trial HyperLink (450 patients, 16 clinics randomised as clusters, meaning whole clinics rather than single patients), pharmacists adjusting drugs from home readings got 57.2% controlled against 30.0% with usual care [D1-01-F04]. An independent US review by the Peterson Health Technology Institute (PHTI, October 2024) rated monitoring-only programmes "not clinically meaningful" and programmes where care teams adjust drugs "clinically meaningful and more rapid", against a threshold of a 5 mmHg fall in systolic pressure [D1-01-F39].

![Extra fall in systolic pressure against usual care, by what was done with the reading](adaptive-care-pictures/20-what-lowers-pressure.png)

*Chart: separate trials with different patients and end points, so compare the groups, not single bars [D1-01-F01, D1-01-F02, D1-01-F03, D1-01-F04, D1-01-F05, D1-01-F06, D1-01-F07].*

**Rule 2. Who is allowed to decide matters more than the electronics.** In the UK trial TASMINH4 (1,182 patients, 12 months), the family doctor adjusted doses in every arm, and sending home readings electronically instead of on paper added 1.2 mmHg, not significant (small enough to be chance) [D1-01-F03]. In rural China, the China Rural Hypertension Control Project (CRHCP: 326 villages, 33,995 people aged 40 and over) let village doctors start and step drugs by a standard protocol under physician supervision. At 18 months, 57.0% were under 130/80 against 19.9% with enhanced usual care [D1-01-F09]. After a median of 4 years, cardiovascular events were lower, with a hazard ratio (a risk ratio over time; below 1 means fewer events) of 0.75 at age 60 and over and 0.64 under 60. The programme also gave free or discounted drugs and paid village doctors incentives tied to control rates, so the effect cannot be credited to the protocol alone [D1-01-F38].

India has the null result that belongs here. mWellcare (40 community health centres, 3,698 people with hypertension, diabetes or both, 12 months, randomised by centre) gave nurses and doctors an app that prompted treatment steps during visits. Against enhanced usual care the systolic difference was 0.98 mmHg, not significant; both arms fell by about 13 mmHg [D1-01-F46]. OUR VIEW: it shows that a prompt at a visit adds nothing to a well-run protocol programme. It does not show that dose changes between visits fail: the app did not act on home readings or reach patients who never came back, and no Indian randomised trial of home readings driving a dose protocol was found (D1-01, NOT FOUND list).

![Share of patients under control with and without a programme that steps the dose](adaptive-care-pictures/21-share-controlled.png)

*Chart: three settings where someone other than a specialist was allowed to step the dose. The China programme also gave free or cheaper medicines and paid for results; the India figures are before and after among the 51% who returned, with no comparison group [D1-01-F04, D1-01-F09, D1-03-F10].*

**Rule 3. Adherence technology alone does not move outcomes.** Smart pillboxes or medication labels for tuberculosis (ASCENT, 23,483 patients, four countries) did not reduce poor outcomes against standard care [D1-02-F59]. Inhaler reminders for 220 New Zealand children raised adherence to 84% against 30% over 6 months, with no difference in school absence [D1-02-F01]. A blood-pressure reminder app (MedISAFE-BP, 411 US adults, 12 weeks) moved systolic pressure by 0.5 mmHg, not significant [D1-01-F06]. OUR VIEW: raising a dose that is not being taken is unsafe, so adherence is an input to the dose decision, not a product.

**Rule 4. Someone must pay for the outcome.** Chapter 1.5 told this side: apps and sensors sold on their own failed, while the firms that lasted are paid by employers and insurers, or sell a device that delivers the therapy.

**Rule 5. People stay when their own clinician is in it.** In eight remote US studies (over 1,00,000 participants), median retention was 5.5 days; referral by a clinician added 40 days, and payment 22 days (one study) [D1-02-F27]. OUR VIEW: a consumer app is the wrong shape.

#### What the loop changes, by kind

| What the loop changes | Home signal | Proof | Who decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Drug dose or protocol step | Cuff, fasting sugar, INR (a blood test of clotting time), weight | Strong | Patient on an agreed plan, pharmacist, nurse or health worker |
| 2. Food and behaviour | Glucose sensor, step count | Mixed | App, coach |
| 3. Exercise as the dose | Camera, pulse, effort rating | Matches in-person care | Therapist or app |
| 4. Therapy delivered by the device (closed-loop insulin pump) | Glucose sensor | Strong | Algorithm, within limits a clinician sets [D1-05-F41] |
| 5. Biofeedback (a body signal shown back to the patient) | Breathing or pulse sensor | Weak | Patient |

OUR VIEW: proof is strongest in kinds 1 and 4, where a drug dose with a known effect is changed and the signal is cheap, valid and fast. Kind 4 needs expensive hardware; kind 1 needs almost none.

#### The evidence by condition

| Condition | What works (headline) | Main caution |
|---|---|---|
| Blood pressure | Dose stepping from home readings by the patient, a pharmacist or a village doctor (rules 1 and 2); strongest proof, up to hard outcomes at 4 years | Leg swelling 32% against 22% with self-titration in TASMINH2 [D1-01-F01]; the China gain came with free drugs and incentives |
| Type 2 diabetes | A device computing the next insulin dose (d-Nav, 181 US adults, 6 months, against clinician support alone): HbA1c down 1.0 point against 0.3, with equal low-sugar events; several authors were maker-linked [D1-01-F13] | Low blood sugar. PHTI (March 2024) found US type 2 tools without a continuous glucose monitor "do not deliver clinically meaningful benefits" [D1-01-F40] |
| Heart failure | Fast dose stepping after discharge (STRONG-HF, 1,078 patients, 14 countries, against usual care): readmission or death at 180 days an adjusted 15.2% against 23.3% [D1-01-F27] | More side effects (41% against 29%), four visits in two months, lab tests. | Asthma | Each reliever puff also raises the steroid dose (SMART): flare-ups risk ratio 0.68 (16 trials, 22,748 people), with no sensor [D1-02-F37] | A sensor inhaler system's benefit interval crossed 1 [D1-02-F02] |

Other areas repeat the pattern. Nurses stepping gout drugs to a target worked with no software at all [D1-02-F06]; tele-rehabilitation matches in-person care but does not beat usual care [D1-02-F03, D1-02-F07]; and guided breathing for blood pressure showed no effect once maker-linked trials were removed [D1-02-F11].

**Prescription apps and safety.** Software sold as a prescribed treatment has passed trials, yet most of the companies behind it failed (chapter 1.5), and none of the prescription apps in the research note changes a drug dose. The closest, Japan's CureApp HT, coaches lifestyle: in 390 untreated adults over 12 weeks it lowered 24-hour systolic pressure 2.4 mmHg more than lifestyle advice alone [D1-01-F07]. OUR VIEW: dose stepping by protocol has a good safety record when the protocol has stop rules. At 4 years in rural China, injurious falls, symptomatic low blood pressure, fainting and kidney outcomes did not differ between groups [D1-01-F38], and insulin-dose proposals in TeleDiab-2 (191 adults, 4 months) caused no severe low blood sugar [D1-01-F14]. The known costs, more minor side effects, leg swelling and low blood sugar, each need their own safety branch.

### 2.2 How care has been scaled without specialists

> **In short.**
> - India's programme already has a protocol, free drugs, a record and an overdue list; the dose step still waits for a doctor's visit.
> - Patients who keep attending do well on the protocol; the loss is among those who stop coming.
> - Abroad, the largest gains came where a non-doctor could step the dose, with free drugs or one pill.
> - Software was part of every working programme and the whole of none.

Chapter 2.1 showed that changing the dose works. This section asks how it has been delivered at scale without specialists.

#### India's hypertension programme in practice

IHCI runs a state protocol of 3 to 4 steps, amlodipine first (chapter 4.1 gives one state's steps); Punjab and Maharashtra order the steps differently at the second step [D3-05-F01, D1-06-F01, D1-06-F02]. Drugs are free, as 30-day refills at every facility level from health and wellness centres to district hospitals [D3-05-F02]. The open-source Simple app takes about 80 seconds to register a patient and 15 seconds for a follow-up, lists overdue patients with high-risk ones first, and offers one-click nurse calls and automated reminders [D3-05-F03]. Medical officers start amlodipine and review at 30 days; nurses register patients and record follow-up [D3-05-F06].

For patients who keep coming, the protocol works: in Punjab and Maharashtra, 70% and 76% of patients seen again were controlled on amlodipine 5 mg alone, and 75% and 81% after step 2 [D3-05-F04]. Across 159,292 patients who returned and stayed on protocol, control on clinic readings plateaued at 77% and 82%; about 43% of starters are missing from that count [D1-06-F04]. The drugs are cheap: amlodipine for the first two steps cut drug cost about 20%, from US$2.50 to US$2.05 per patient per year, not counting staff [D3-05-F05].

OUR VIEW: IHCI already has the protocol, free drugs, a record and an overdue list. It lacks a dose decision for the uncontrolled patient who does not go back to the doctor (chapter 1.3).

**Who changes the dose in practice.** Besides the refill rule in chapter 1.3, search summaries (only) say that state protocol text has the medical officer start and raise the dose, while the ANM (auxiliary nurse midwife) gives a month's medicines once pressure is controlled [D3-05-F31], and that a CHO (community health officer) screens, refers and follows up [D3-05-F30, D3-05-F13]. Whether a nurse or health officer may change a dose in law is open (chapter 2.6). OUR VIEW: design for a doctor approving every dose change.

**Tamil Nadu's doorstep scheme.** Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam, launched in August 2021, reported 1,86,13,872 beneficiaries by August 2024, with 92,59,821 people with hypertension given medicines (government figures, not audited; THEIR CLAIM) [D3-05-F14]. It refills; it does not step: no source says the dose is changed at the door [D3-05-F15]. OUR VIEW: high treatment with low control (chapter 1.2) is the gap a dose step addresses.

#### Programmes outside India

| Programme, size | Who steps the dose | Also in the package | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| China rural project (CRHCP) | Village doctors by protocol, physician-supervised | Free or discounted drugs; pay on control rate; audit; free home cuffs (search summary only) [D3-05-F21] | Chapter 2.1 |
| Lesotho; 103 villages, 547 people | Lay village health workers, by app | One fixed-dose pill | Under 140/90 at 12 months 58% against 48% (cluster trial against facility referral) [D3-05-F24] |
| IMPACT-BP, South Africa; 774 people | Nurses, remotely by app (not stated explicitly) | Health worker collects readings at home and delivers medicine | Systolic 7.9-9.1 mmHg lower at 6 months against standard care [D3-05-F27] |
| Nigeria; 60 centres, 21,922 people | Health workers under standing orders (search summary only) | Free drugs, then a US$1.60 co-pay a month; single pills; steady stock | Control 21.7% to 55.9% (2020-2023), no comparison group [D3-05-F22, D3-05-F23] |
| COBRA-BPS, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Sri Lanka; 2,645 people | Trained physicians; health workers monitored and referred | Home visits, counselling | Systolic 5.2 mmHg lower at 24 months (cluster trial) [D1-01-F37] |
| CARRS, India and Pakistan; 1,146 people with diabetes | Physicians, with software prompts | Care coordinators calling monthly | Combined target 18.2% against 8.7% at 28 months [D3-05-F18] (another note gives 8.1% for the comparison arm [D2-03-F14]); the chance of improving HbA1c, blood pressure and LDL cholesterol together doubled, risk ratio 2.1 at 12 months [D1-01-F23] |

OUR VIEW: the largest gains in control came where a non-doctor could step the dose, with free drugs or one pill; monitoring, counselling or prompts gained less or nothing. What these programmes changed, and software alone could not, was drug stock, authority for a non-doctor to change drugs (the PAHO HEARTS roadmap across 33 countries names nurse dose adjustment [D3-05-F28]), a person carrying medicine home, pay linked to control, and a person who phones and books visits. Software was part of every working programme and the whole of none.

### 2.3 What the technology can and cannot do

> **In short.**
> - A home reading can be several mmHg wrong for reasons unrelated to the cuff, such as posture; trusting it is the hard engineering.
> - Every working dose algorithm caps its actions and falls back to a standing plan when data is missing.
> - Language models make dose errors even when told to follow a protocol; they can read and speak, but should not decide.
> - Learning each patient's response is later work: no open data or simulator exists for blood-pressure drugs.

If changing the treatment is what works, the technology questions narrow to two: can a home reading be trusted, and can a decision be kept within safe bounds. OUR VIEW: the hard engineering is in trusting the home reading and in bounding the decision, not in the model that makes it.

#### Trusting a home reading

The American and European hypertension societies publish rules for home readings: how long to rest, how many readings, over how many days, and a lower threshold at home than in clinic; chapter 4.3 applies them [D2-02-F10, D2-02-F11, D2-01-F1]. Small mistakes move the number:

| Mistake | Effect, systolic / diastolic (mmHg) |
|---|---|
| Arm on the lap instead of on a desk | +3.9 / +4.0 (randomised crossover trial, 133 people, Baltimore) [D2-02-F16] |
| Arm hanging at the side | +6.5 / +4.4 (same trial) |
| Back not supported; legs crossed | +5 to +15 / +6; +5 to +8 / +3 to +5 (American Heart Association) [D2-02-F18] |
| Regular cuff on an arm that needs a large or an extra-large one | +4.8 or +19.5 systolic (randomised crossover trial, 195 people) [D2-02-F19] |

Noise has already hurt an Indian trial. SMARThealth India (a stepped-wedge cluster trial, in which groups of patients start the programme one after another; 18 clusters, rural Andhra Pradesh) found no blood-pressure effect, partly because "fluctuating BP values" made risk scores disagree, so only 37.2% of the cohort was exposed [D2-03-F17].

Getting the number in is also hard. No cuff sold in India was confirmed to use the standard Bluetooth blood-pressure service [D2-02-F3]. A purpose-trained reader of cuff-screen photos reached mean errors of 1.2/0.8 mmHg on 776 US images, but 82% of 7,205 images in a Guatemalan set were poor quality, and only Omron screens were tested [D2-02-F4]. No published test of a general vision-language model on these screens was found [D2-02-F8]. A Bluetooth home cuff, the Omron HEM-7156T, costs about ₹2,600 (2026 listings ₹2,593-3,500; search summary only) [D1-06-F31]. Cuffless blood pressure is not ready: the FDA cleared one cuffless monitor in July 2025 [D2-02-F23], but the European Society of Hypertension says there are "no generally accepted protocols" for validating such devices [D2-02-F25].

OUR VIEW: blood pressure is the one signal a household can measure with a validated, cheap device. Cuffless error is about the size of a dose step, so dose decisions stay on cuff readings, and every photo read must be shown back to the patient to confirm.

#### Algorithms that set a dose

Every dose algorithm in use is bounded. The do-it-yourself insulin system OpenAPS writes its safety rules down: it acts only through temporary basal rates, so if it stops, the pump returns to the normal plan; the rate is capped by the lowest of several limits, including "4x the current scheduled basal rate"; and missing or conflicting data sends it back to the pre-set plan [D2-03-F46]. The American Diabetes Association's 2025 insulin rule steps the dose every 3 days towards a fasting target and lowers it 10-20% after an unexplained low [D1-06-F16]; India's ICMR workflow gives targets but no titration schedule [D1-06-F17]. Blood-pressure ladders use fixed steps with a 30-day review, lab and pregnancy guards and referral at the end (chapter 4.1) [D1-06-F14].

People do differ. PHYSIC (randomised, double-blind, repeated crossover, 280 people, Sweden) found real between-person differences in drug response, worth about 4.4 mmHg if the drug were chosen per person [D2-03-F12], while a one-time "responder" label did not predict 12-month response in 608 Chinese patients [D2-03-F13]. OUR VIEW: every working dose algorithm caps its actions and falls back to a standing plan when data is missing; a ladder is the simplest case, and PHYSIC is the honest reason a later learning layer could add value.

#### A protocol as data, and decision support in India

A protocol written as data, not code, can be read by a clinician, tested branch by branch and swapped without changing the engine. No ready standard fits: WHO SMART Guidelines have no general hypertension kit [D2-03-F50]. Testing is the hard part: when the US VA re-encoded its hypertension decision support (ATHENA-HTN), 26 decision points gave 3,120 possible outputs, and 100 test cases covered "only 1% of the possible output combinations" [D2-03-F55].

Indian trials of decision support for clinicians are mixed. CARRS (chapter 2.2) was positive with care coordinators; mWellcare (chapter 2.1) [D2-03-F15] and SMARThealth (above) were null. IMPACT Diabetes (cluster randomised, 418 people, 9 months) gave ASHAs (village health workers) a tablet tool under physician supervision: HbA1c fell by 0.5 or more in 21.8% against 10.3% with usual care [D2-03-F18, D3-04-F17]. OUR VIEW: decision support alone did not beat a well-run programme in two Indian trials, so an engine must act between visits, not prompt during them, and ATHENA's 1% says to test on generated cases and replayed data, not only hand-written ones.

#### What language models may and may not do with doses

GPT-4, prompted with a hospital tacrolimus protocol on 300 simulated cases, made 13.9% "sentinel errors" in its first version; with stronger constraints and output checks the rate fell to 1.0%, and 13.3% of runs were not reproducible (OUR ARITHMETIC: 100 - 86.7) [D2-03-F38]. No completed randomised trial of a language-model coach with a blood-pressure or HbA1c outcome was found; two are registered [D2-03-F44]. Of the health models the large labs have built, only MedGemma, PaPaGei and HealthBench can be downloaded, and none sets doses [D2-03-F32, D2-03-F28, D2-03-F33].

OUR VIEW, following [D2-03-F38, D2-03-F40, D2-03-F46]: the model handles language only (reading the cuff photo, translation, speech); a fixed, tested engine makes every decision from the protocol file; code checks every output against the protocol; and a clinician approves every change.

#### Later work

Adaptive nudging, which learns when and what to send each person, has one head-to-head win, on steps, not on a clinical measure (DIAMANTE, 168 people, US) [D2-03-F10]. A "digital twin" (a model of one person that predicts their response) is further off for blood-pressure drugs. No open model of that response, no titration simulator, no open type 2 diabetes simulator and no large open dataset of home readings with medicine changes was found [D1-06-F42, D1-06-F43, D1-06-F30]. What can be tested on now is the programme's control-by-step figures [D1-06-F04] and open home blood-pressure series (the R `bp` package, GPL-3) [D2-03-F61]. OUR VIEW: each approved step in a loop gives one labelled example, readings before and 30 days after, which is the dataset a response model would need.

### 2.4 Who is already doing this

> **In short.**
> - In India, many programmes coach around a sensor; the one product found that changes a dose is a drug maker's insulin app of unknown status.
> - Abroad, lasting businesses are paid by employers, insurers or health systems, or sell a device that delivers the dose.
> - The nearest relatives of a home dose loop are a US company whose software is in a regulator's pilot, a UK company now wound up, and a Japanese cuff maker's UK tool.
> - Most money abroad goes to products that show data or coach; no funded home blood-pressure dose-step service was found.

Scaled companies abroad are paid by employers, insurers or health systems, or sell a device that delivers the therapy (chapter 1.5 gave Hinge Health and Insulet). Omada (US), paid by employers, plans and health systems, had revenue of $91.3M from 1,134,000 members in the quarter to 30 Sep 2026 and is listed and profitable [D1-05-F12, D1-05-F13]. #### The nearest relatives

No funded startup of 2025 or 2026 was found whose funding page describes home blood-pressure dose steps by protocol. The nearest are these.

- **Cadence (US).** Remote vital signs for older adults; supervised AI agents support medicine changes under the partner medical group's protocols, and health systems pay; how far the AI decides alone is not stated. It raised a $100M Series C in June 2026 [D2-06-F1] and reports 100,000+ active patients and blood-pressure control up 70% relative (THEIR CLAIM) [D2-06-F2]. - **Closed Loop Medicine (UK).** Its one blood-pressure trial (published 2024, company and university authors) adjusted amlodipine through an app in 205 adults over 14 weeks, with no comparison group; systolic fell 11 mmHg [D3-06-F09]. It also launched GLP-1 dose options for clinicians in the US (WeDosify, 18 Sep 2025) [D2-06-F24]. The company has been in members' voluntary liquidation (a solvent winding-up by shareholders) since 28 Apr 2026 [D3-06-F07].
- **Omron (Japan), in the UK.** The cuff maker's Hypertension Plus collates home readings and drafts a medication plan that the clinician accepts or modifies (search summary only; THEIR CLAIM) [D3-06-F35].

#### Prescribed apps and funded companies abroad

Germany is the one country with years of numbers on apps that doctors prescribe and public insurers pay for (called DiGA): about 1.9 million prescriptions in 2020-2025 [D1-05-F27], about EUR 400M paid by insurers in five years [D1-05-F28], and an average negotiated price of EUR 227 across 40 apps [D1-05-F29]; 18 of 81 listed apps were removed for lack of proven benefit (Sep 2026) [D1-05-F25]. Japan insures CureApp HT (chapter 2.6), but its prescription counts were NOT FOUND [D1-05-F36]. OUR VIEW: public payers will pay for prescribed apps, but increasingly for results.

Among 32 companies funded or launched abroad, the largest rounds went to Function ($298M), Hippocratic AI ($126M), Cadence and Nourish ($100M each), and only Cadence describes medicine changes [D2-06-F42, D2-06-F20, D2-06-F5]. Blood-pressure money buys sensors, not decisions: Biobeat, Aktiia and Vital Signals raised $50M, $42M and $15M+ for products that only show readings [D2-06-F36, D2-06-F37, D2-06-F38]. Only three of the 32 are clearly at depth 4 (Cadence, Twin Health and WeDosify). The frontier AI labs' consumer health features of 2026, such as ChatGPT Health (7 Jan) and Claude's health connectors (11 Jan), read phone health data and change no treatment [D2-06-F15, D2-06-F16]. None of the Y Combinator and a16z lines read mentions dose titration by rule [D2-06-F47, D2-06-F49, D2-06-F50].

OUR VIEW, the gap: abroad the pattern exists inside broad virtual clinics paid by US payers and for weight-loss medicines, and the low-income-country programmes found are protocol care, not device-linked dose loops [D1-05-F65]. Nobody funded sells a blood-pressure dose loop for a public programme run by health workers. These are one night's search results: a hospital, drug maker or state may run something unpublished.

### 2.5 The money

> **In short.**
> - Most money in blood-pressure care goes to medicines; a service priced near a pill would double the patient's bill, so usually someone else must pay.
> - No Indian payer was found for remote monitoring or digital therapy; our view is that a private clinic or hospital chain is the likeliest first payer.
> - On our own arithmetic the reachable Indian market is real but small for a venture fund, and the US$100 million-a-year test fails on India alone.
> - Indian chronic-care firms in the notes lose money; the profitable foreign ones are paid by employers and plans.

Chapter 1.5 showed who pays today. This section follows the rupee further.

#### What is spent today

India's retail medicine market was ₹2,45,943 crore in the year to March 2026 [D2-05-F02]. Blood-pressure medicines sold about ₹16,371 crore at retail chemists in the year to December 2025 (not hospital tenders or Jan Aushadhi; the page does not label the period explicitly) [D2-05-F01]. ### 2.6 The rules

> **In short.**
> - India's July 2026 guidance treats software that guides treatment as a medical device; a doctor-approved dose proposal could fall in Class A, B or C, which the regulator confirms.
> - In telemedicine, only a registered doctor may prescribe, and must deliver the prescription directly.
> - A study on patients needs permission and ethics approval first.
> - Abroad, each country pays through a clinician, and Germany ties part of the price to results.

These are readings of the texts; open points remain questions for a lawyer and for CDSCO, India's medical-device regulator.

#### Software as a medical device

CDSCO issued its "Guidance Document on Medical Device Software" on 21 Jul 2026 [D2-04-F01]. India has four risk classes, A lowest and D highest, and the class follows the intended use stated in the label, website and promotion [D2-04-F02]. The grid for standalone software [D2-04-F03]:

| Situation | Treatment or diagnosis | Drive clinical management | Inform clinical management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical | D | C | B |
| Serious | C | B | A |
| Non-serious | B | A | A |

"Drive clinical management" covers information "to guide next diagnostics or next treatment interventions" [D2-04-F04]. A dose proposal that a doctor approves reads most naturally as "drive": Class B if hypertension is "serious", Class A if "non-serious", Class C if CDSCO reads it as "treatment" [D2-04-F06, D2-04-F49]. The guidance does not say where hypertension sits [D2-04-F05], and a patient-facing tool used without a professional can be moved up to "critical". CDSCO names a hypertension digital-therapeutics platform as medical device software [D2-04-F07], and India has no exemption for tools only a clinician sees [D2-04-F10]. OUR VIEW: clinician sign-off lowers the class; it does not take the software out of the device rules.

#### Testing on patients

A test licence (Form MD-13) covers making small quantities "for the purpose of Clinical Investigations or Test or Evaluation or Demonstration" [D2-04-F13]. A clinical investigation on patients needs CDSCO permission (MD-22/MD-23) [D2-04-F14], which the flowchart says is not mandatory for Class A [D2-04-F39]. Whether a pilot in which software drafts and a doctor decides counts as one is a question for counsel [D2-04-F50]. Trials must be registered on CTRI before the first patient, with ethics approval uploaded; whether a pilot in which the clinician decides needs this was NOT FOUND (search summary only) [D2-04-F45]. Fees and processing days were NOT FOUND [D2-04-F16].

#### Who may prescribe and change a dose

The Telemedicine Practice Guidelines (25 Mar 2020), clause 5.4, say platforms based on artificial intelligence or machine learning "are not allowed to counsel the patients or prescribe any medicines"; they may "assist and support" the doctor, but only a registered doctor may counsel or prescribe, and "the final prescription or counseling has to be directly delivered by the RMP" (registered medical practitioner) [D2-04-F17]. Whether later rules changed this in 2026 was not checked. In the national hypertension programme, community health officers delivered refills "based on the patient's prior prescription", and no law, rule or ministry order was found that lets a nurse, health officer or pharmacist change a dose under a standing order [D2-04-F18]. The Drugs and Magic Remedies Act 1954 bans advertisements suggesting a drug treats "High or low blood pressure", among other listed conditions; whether software counts as a "drug" under it is untested [D2-04-F31].

OUR VIEW, for tonight's prototype: it is for test and demonstration, not a device on sale and not tested on patients; it uses open data, simulated patients or teammates' readings; a clinician approves every change and delivers the prescription, while the software only drafts; and it makes no claim that it treats or controls high blood pressure.

### 2.7 The biology underneath (new research, 14 Nov)

> **In short.**
> - Each protocol drug pulls a different lever, so adding a drug does far more than raising a dose, and step 4 adds almost nothing.
> - Every drug is at a steady level well before the day 24-30 readings, so the 30-day step fits the biology.
> - A step moves pressure less than the natural noise between two monthly means, so the loop must judge where the mean sits, with enough days, not how far it moved.
> - Each safety check has a mechanism and a time window; the body gives no cheap sign that amlodipine or telmisartan was taken.

Sections 2.1 to 2.6 rest on trial results and policy texts. This section, researched on 14 November after the rest of the document, adds the physiology underneath: why a step works, how fast, how much readings move on their own, why each safety check exists, and how to tell whether a medicine was taken. It is new research, and its main facts were re-checked by a second agent.

**Three levers.** Amlodipine relaxes the muscle of the smallest arteries [D4-01-F17]. Telmisartan blocks angiotensin II, the hormone that tightens them and drives aldosterone [D4-01-F20]. Chlorthalidone makes the kidney pass more salt and water [D4-01-F23]. The families add up, and partly cancel each other's counter-reactions: a thiazide raises renin and aldosterone, a counter-response that the ARB (angiotensin receptor blocker) blocks [D4-01-F22]. So doubling a dose gives only about one fifth of the extra fall that adding a second family gives [D4-01-F09]. Priced along the Gujarat ladder from the US combination label (1,461 patients, 8 weeks), step 2 adds about 5.3 mmHg systolic, step 3 about 4.0 and step 4 only 1.7 (OUR ARITHMETIC) [D4-01-F49]; in 947 patients not controlled on amlodipine 10 mg, telmisartan 80 mg did 0.2 mmHg better than 40 mg [D4-01-F50]. Step 2 has a cost: ankle swelling rises from 3.0% at 5 mg to 10.8% at 10 mg [D4-01-F19], and adding telmisartan to amlodipine 5 mg instead lowered pressure more, with swelling in 4.3% against 27.2% [D4-01-F51].

**Who responds to what.** People do differ, but mainly between two groups: a renin-angiotensin blocker on one side, amlodipine or a thiazide on the other (chapter 2.3 gave PHYSIC's size) [D4-01-F02, D4-01-F04]. Salt shifts the odds: a salty diet blunts renin-angiotensin blockers more than calcium blockers [D4-01-F13], and in north-east India 47.6% of people with high pressure were salt-sensitive [D4-01-F47]. For South Asians, 16 small trials, mostly Indian, found no difference between families [D4-01-F11]. Gene tests are not usable: the best single variant moves response by about 3 mmHg, and polygenic scores predict nothing [D4-01-F35, D4-01-F34]. And one patient's change after one step is mostly noise: in 28,281 trial patients only 3% of the apparent spread in response was real [D4-03-F55].

**How fast a step works.** Amlodipine reaches a steady blood level in 7-8 days [D4-02-F01], telmisartan in about 4-5 and chlorthalidone in about 7-12 (OUR ARITHMETIC from their half-lives) [D4-02-F05, D4-02-F08]. Across 18 trials, half of a first drug's full effect arrived in about a week [D4-02-F36]. On an assumed curve shape, readings on days 24-30 see about 80-96% of the full effect, and a 14-day step would see only 48-78% (OUR ARITHMETIC; the paper's equation could not be checked) [D4-02-F37]. Telmisartan's labels disagree on the maximum, about 4 weeks in the US and 4-8 weeks in a European text [D4-02-F06, D4-02-F43], and in a small Indian trial chlorthalidone's 24-hour effect was still growing at 12 weeks [D4-02-F44]. Punjab's sheet already allows 15-day steps "if required" [D4-02-F15]; Madhya Pradesh's does not [D4-02-F16]. Faster stepping has mostly observational support [D4-02-F25], and in the one trial with side-effect numbers (Nigeria, 300 adults) the faster arm had more early kidney-function falls and low potassium (corrected in the claims check) [D4-02-F34]. OUR VIEW: 30 days is right for the Gujarat steps; a shorter step is defensible only for amlodipine 5 to 10 mg.

**How much readings move on their own.** A single home reading in treated patients varies with an SD (standard deviation) of 11.8 mmHg within a week [D4-03-F10]. Averaging helps: between two weekly means in untreated adults, a real change must be about 12.5 mmHg for 3-day means and 8.5 mmHg for 7-day means (OUR ARITHMETIC) [D4-03-F34], and treated older patients vary more (claims check). A step's 2-6 mmHg is smaller than this, so the loop should judge where the mean sits against 135/85, with 7 days when it is near the line [D4-03-F02], not how far it moved. UK self-titration trials changed a drug only after two consecutive months above target [D4-03-F13, D4-03-F56]. Indian winter readings ran 9-11 mmHg above summer, though these were readings by nurses or investigators, not home cuffs [D4-03-F31, D4-03-F32], and patients picked for a high reading drift down on their own (regression to the mean) [D4-03-F26].

**Why each safety check exists.** Telmisartan relaxes the vessel leaving each kidney filter, so filtration pressure falls and creatinine rises a little, mostly in the first 2 weeks [D4-04-F26, D4-04-F27]. The UK Kidney Association rechecks creatinine and potassium 1-2 weeks after every ARB start or dose rise [D4-04-F34]; the Indian state sheets opened ask for no recheck [D4-04-F57]. Even rises under 30% went with higher death rates in UK records, though they may mark sicker patients [D4-04-F22]. High potassium can stop the heart, and the ECG can look normal [D4-04-F35]. In pregnancy an ARB stops the fetus making urine in the second and third trimesters, so the amniotic fluid drains and the lungs cannot grow [D4-04-F02, D4-04-F56]; NICE says stop within 2 working days and switch [D4-04-F29]. Thiazides cause low sodium most often in the first 30 days and in older, lighter people [D4-04-F58, D4-04-F61]. Among people over 60, 22.2% have a pressure drop on standing [D4-04-F39], and hip fractures rise in the 45 days after starting any blood-pressure drug [D4-04-F48]. A reading of 180/110 without symptoms is urgency, not emergency, which means organ damage [D4-04-F70]: patients sent home had no major heart events in 7 days [D4-04-F68], and long-standing high pressure resets the brain's protection of its blood flow to higher pressures [D4-04-F72] (OUR VIEW: an argument against lowering it fast at home).

**Lab tests and signs of intake.** The tests that move within a 30-day cycle are creatinine and potassium, plus sodium at the thiazide step; HbA1c moves over 2-3 months, and NT-proBNP is a district-hospital test for suspected heart failure [D4-05-F37, D4-05-F35]. A primary health centre runs creatinine, urea and uric acid, but sends potassium and urine albumin to a hub [D4-05-F39]. On adherence, drug assays abroad found about 1 in 3 patients with resistant hypertension not taking their drugs (31.2% pooled) [D4-05-F01], and a Brazilian clinic found 90.1% adherent by questionnaire against 32.4% by urine test [D4-05-F07]. No Indian assay study was found [D4-05-F15]. Amlodipine and telmisartan leave no mark on the pulse [D4-05-F16, D4-05-F18]; only the thiazide leaves a weak chemical trace, lower potassium and higher uric acid [D4-05-F22]. Cheap reminder devices did nothing for 53,480 US patients [D4-05-F24], while blister packs raised pills taken by 11% [D4-05-F46].

OUR VIEW: the biology fits the loop's shape and points to five additions: expected effects per step, profile fields, adherence checks beyond self-report, a reading rule sized to the noise, and a safety window after each step. Chapter 4.9 lists them as product features, and Appendix I holds the full detail.
