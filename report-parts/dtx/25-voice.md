## C2. What patients and doctors say in public

### What this part is and is not

The team has done no interviews, so this part stands in until it does. It reports published interview studies, surveys, public app reviews and posts, labelled PUBLISHED STUDY, PUBLIC REVIEW or FORUM POST. None of it is customer evidence gathered by the team; reviews are self-selected and studies were read as abstracts only.

### Why patients miss follow-up and stop medicines

Eight Indian interview studies (PUBLISHED STUDY) name the same causes: feeling well, cost, family duties, fear of long-term drugs, traditional medicine.

| Where | People | Barriers, in the authors' words |
|---|---|---|
| Coastal Karnataka, tertiary hospital | 32 hypertensive patients lost to follow-up | "symptom-driven healthcare seeking, medication non-adherence, occupational responsibilities, and competing family priorities"; wage loss, rushed consultations [D3-04-F01] |
| Rural Tamil Nadu (Thiruvallur) | 15 adults with diabetes or hypertension | Stopping is structural, unintentional (work, travel, caregiving) or intentional, "perceived harm, symptoms or treatment need"; "Family relationships could both support and hinder medication-taking" [D3-04-F02] |
| Tehri, Uttarakhand | 40 (20 diabetes, 20 hypertension) | "the use of alternative medicine"; "lack of self-management guidance from their physicians" [D3-04-F03] |

Karnataka patients suggested reminders and digital records [D3-04-F01]. A FORUM POST by an adult child says the father's pressure stayed controlled on Ayurvedic treatment, then rose, and he "re-started some medication" [D3-04-F52].

So what for us (OUR VIEW): feeling well recurs as a reason to stop, and a home reading is one thing that can contradict it.

### Home monitors in Indian homes

Few patients own a home cuff, most taught themselves, and home readings show that many "controlled" patients are not.

- **Who has one.** Of 2,588 clinic-attending people with hypertension and caregivers in North India, 18.1% used a home monitor (urban 20.5%, rural 8.5%) [D3-04-F06]. No household figure exists; a web claim of under 5% cites no source [D3-04-F22].
- **Who taught them.** 75.2% reported "learning HBPM themselves through observation, videos, and reading"; 24.7% were trained by a health worker. Technique was excellent in 15.9% of patients and 5.9% of caregivers [D3-04-F06].
- **Do doctors act on home readings.** About 88% of 832 Indian doctors recommend home monitoring, but estimate only 30.4% of patients measure; 31.3% say to measure before tablets, about 30% give no instructions [D3-04-F07]. Only 15.1% knew the correct home thresholds [D3-04-F18].
- **What home readings reveal.** In GRAND (18 centres, 12 states, 1,920 treated regular attenders, 7 days of home readings), 77.7% looked controlled by clinic pressure but 55.9% by clinic plus home; 21.8% had masked uncontrolled pressure (high at home, normal in clinic) [D3-04-F09]. Median knowledge of home monitoring was 1 out of 7 [D3-04-F10].

So what for us (OUR VIEW): the loop must bring the cuff, the teaching and the thresholds.

### What doctors and health workers say

Doctors describe heavy workloads, and many private doctors do not follow the state protocol.

- **Workload.** In Kolar, Karnataka: "Doctors face very high workloads, especially in the public sector"; care is "very doctor-centred" [D3-04-F16]. Meghalaya reports staff vacancies and limited training [D3-04-F12, D3-04-F20].
- **The state protocol.** Of 30 private doctors in Bhopal, 19 did not follow it, citing "fear of either losing patients due to a lack of immediate blood pressure control or causing drug-related adverse effects" and a preference for fixed-dose combinations (two drugs in one tablet) [D3-04-F15].
- **Health workers with a decision tool.** IMPACT Diabetes, a cluster trial in 16 villages in two states (418 people, 9 months): ASHAs (village health workers) with a tablet tool under physician supervision, against usual care. HbA1c (average blood sugar over about three months) fell by 0.5 or more in 21.8% against 10.3%; physician visits 85.0% against 29.8%; "acceptable and feasible for patients, ASHAs, and physicians" [D3-04-F17]. Diabetes, not blood pressure.

Not found: any Indian doctor, nurse or health worker on dose steps from home readings, or any qualitative study of ASHAs on blood-pressure follow-up.

So what for us (OUR VIEW): a doctor's approval cannot be assumed.

### Reminders and messages

In a Punjab trial at two public hospitals, 388 patients with uncontrolled pressure got two myth-busting WhatsApp reminders before their visit or none; attendance was 21.8% against 19.6%, a null result, and beliefs did not change [D3-04-F50]. A Delhi study of 110 patients (counselling, voice calls, SMS) reported control rising from 14.7% to 52.9%, with no control group [D3-04-F51].
The phone is often not the patient's. Caregivers enable access and devices are shared [D3-04-F05]; one review moves a meter from a husband's phone to a mother's [D3-04-F25]. 54% of women aged 15-49 own the phone they use, 46.6% in rural areas [D3-04-F23]. Among 150 rural older adults near Mysore, 50% owned a phone, few a smartphone, and digital literacy was 11% [D3-04-F56]. About 10.6% of Indians spoke English in 2011 [D3-04-F57].

So what for us (OUR VIEW): a message only works if it reaches the person who holds the phone and leads to a decision.

### Two field voices from people who run programmes

Both are LinkedIn posts, paraphrased; numbers inside them are unverified.

- A national NCD (non-communicable disease) consultant at UNICEF India, about Apr 2026: programmes failed on BP machines uncalibrated for months and mid-month drug stock-outs; one protocol, steady supply and the Simple app made missed visits visible. Claims control rose 48 percentage points in primary care and 23 in secondary (POST CLAIM, source not named) (LinkedIn, read 13 Nov 2026).
- A doctor and WHO consultant, about Oct 2026: counting visits is not enough; programmes must know who continues, who is controlled and who missed follow-up. Cites mWellcare: a digital system does not guarantee results (LinkedIn, read 13 Nov 2026).

Neither describes dose changes from home readings.

### What we could not find

A household figure for cuff ownership; any Indian clinician voice on dose steps from home readings; any patient voice on a dose increase; voice against text preference among older patients; Reddit posts (the archive timed out) and Google Play reviews.

### So what for us

OUR VIEW:
- **Assume most patients do not own a cuff.** Supply one and teach the technique; only 18.1% used one even among clinic attenders [D3-04-F06].
- **A doctor may not trust the state protocol.** Each proposal must show why the step is safe, answering the fears of slow control and side effects [D3-04-F15].
- **Never rely on a reminder alone.** WhatsApp reminders did not raise attendance [D3-04-F50]; the reading must lead to a decision.
- **Design for the household phone.** Reach whoever holds it, by voice, in the local language [D3-04-F05, D3-04-F23].
- **Risk: home readings bring bad news.** 21.8% of treated clinic attenders had masked uncontrolled pressure [D3-04-F09]; expect fear of more drugs and the urge to stop [D3-04-F02].
