Say: Every bed runs the same loop. A camera reads whatever monitor the hospital already has. The software proposes the next step, but only from a written protocol. For routine steps, the doctor on site approves it in one tap and the nurse does it. Only exceptions go to the remote specialist: a hard stop the protocol names, or anything off the protocol. The specialist approves, changes the step or sends the patient on, and the on-site doctor confirms. So most steps never need a specialist, which is how one specialist can cover many beds. How to read the diagram: - Black path (1 to 4, repeating): routine steps. No specialist involved. The specialist can see the log but is not asked. - Orange path: an exception leaves step 2, goes to the remote specialist, and the decision comes back to the on-site doctor (step 3) to confirm before the nurse acts. What counts as an exception (OUR DESIGN, set in each protocol file): - A hard stop the protocol names. Snakebite: paralysis needing a ventilator, 30 vials given, no ventilator or dialysis on site. Poisoning: breathing failure, signs of atropine toxicity. Sepsis: low BP despite a vasopressor, breathing failure. Heart attack: shock, failed clot-busting. (Idea check, section 5.) - Targets still missed after the steps the file allows. - The on-site doctor wants to leave the protocol, or a reading the protocol does not cover. Why the on-site doctor signs routine steps: - Every step must be a doctor’s order. No Indian rule lets a nurse change a dose on a standing order (D2-04-F18). Under the Telemedicine Guidelines 2020, the doctor who asks for a teleconsult stays the treating doctor (D6-03-F05, search summary only). So routine steps are the on-site doctor’s tap, and a specialist’s call is confirmed by the on-site doctor. (Idea check, section 7.) - Open question for counsel: can the specialist’s approval stand as the order on its own? Other notes: - Camera reading of monitors: a preprint read heart rate, SpO2 and arterial BP from screens with above 98.9% accuracy on 6,498 images. D6-02-F18 (preprint). - Product rule: propose, never act. The software never drives a pump (CDSCO Class C reading, D2-04-F03). - Coverage target: 80+ patients per specialist, against Cloudphysician’s reported 60 to 80 (D6-02-F04, search summary only). That only works if specialists see exceptions, not every step.
