# DT2 spine: the argument every part of the document hangs on (coordinator, Fable, 2026-11-13)

## 1. The thesis

In chronic disease, outcomes improve when a home reading comes back as a changed treatment within days. Readings that only get displayed, and reminders that only nag, change little. The scarce thing in India is the prescriber's decision, which today waits for a clinic visit that half the patients never make. So the product is a way to make safe, protocol-true treatment changes between visits, with a clinician approving each one.

## 2. The challenge, phrase by phrase

| The fund's words | What a product must do (OUR VIEW) |
|---|---|
| "rapid feedback from medical devices or biomarkers" | Use a signal that moves within days of a treatment change and can be measured at home cheaply and correctly. |
| "continuously personalise care" | Recompute the treatment for this patient from this patient's readings: where they are on the protocol, how they responded to the last step, whether they took the medicine, what is unsafe for them. |
| "adaptive to patient needs between appointments" | Change the treatment without waiting for the next visit. |
| "measurable improvements in health outcomes" | Name one endpoint that can move in 90 days and has an accepted threshold for "clinically meaningful" (the US review used a 5 mmHg systolic fall, D1-01-F39). |
| "patients who cannot access frequent specialist support" | Move the routine decision down to a general doctor, nurse or health worker under a written protocol. The specialist owns the protocol and sees the exceptions. |
| "beyond monitoring and reminders" | Reach adaptive depth 4 on the scale in D0-brief: the therapy itself changes. |

## 3. The map of feedback-driven therapies

Grouped by what the loop changes:

1. **Drug dose or protocol step.** Blood pressure (cuff), insulin (glucose), heart failure (weight, pressure, pulse, lab values), warfarin (INR), gout (urate), asthma (reliever use, peak flow), weight-loss injections (weight, side effects).
2. **Food and behaviour.** Glucose-sensor-guided meals; activity nudges.
3. **Exercise as the dose.** Cardiac, lung, stroke and joint rehabilitation guided by camera or pulse.
4. **Therapy delivered by the device itself.** Closed-loop insulin pumps; auto-adjusting breathing machines; nerve stimulation.
5. **Biofeedback.** Breathing or heart-rhythm feedback shown to the patient.

OUR VIEW: the proof is strongest in groups 1 and 4, where the thing being changed is a drug dose with a known effect and the signal is cheap, valid and fast. Group 4 needs expensive hardware. Group 1 needs almost none. That is where a student team, and a low-resource country, should start.

## 4. Five rules the evidence gives

1. **The reading must change the treatment.** Self-monitoring alone 1.0 mmHg; with intensive support that included dose changes, education or counselling, 6.1 (D1-01-F05). Heart-failure telemonitoring without a mandated dose protocol failed twice (D1-01-F32, F33) while fast dose stepping cut death or readmission by a third (D1-01-F27). Warfarin self-testing with a call to the clinic did not cut deaths; self-dosing did, each against usual care in separate trials (D1-02-F05). The US review backed only medication-adjusting programmes (D1-01-F39).
2. **Who is allowed to decide matters more than the electronics.** In TASMINH4, telemonitoring added nothing significant over paper once the GP was titrating (D1-01-F03). Village health workers with authority to step doses got 57.0% controlled against 19.9% (D1-01-F09). Nurses titrating gout medicine reached target in 95% against 30% (D1-02-F06). An Indian app that prompted clinicians during visits added nothing (D1-01-F46).
3. **Adherence technology alone does not move outcomes** (D1-02-F59, D1-02-F01, D1-01-F06). But raising a dose for a patient who is not taking the current one is unsafe and wasteful. So adherence is an input to the dose decision, not a product.
4. **Someone must pay for the outcome.** Companies that sold an app or a sensor on its own died (D1-02-F42, F44, F41). The ones that reached scale are paid by employers and insurers for programmes with clinicians in them (D1-05-F12 to F16), or sell a device that delivers the therapy (D1-05-F41, F42).
5. **People stay when their own clinician is in it.** Median retention in remote studies is 5.5 days; referral by a clinician adds 40 days (D1-02-F27). A consumer app is the wrong shape.

## 5. Why blood pressure first

OUR VIEW, with the facts behind it:
- The largest pool: 35.5% of Indian adults aged 20 and over (D1-03-F01).
- The cheapest valid signal: a home cuff at about ₹2,600 (D1-06-F31, SNIPPET prices).
- The simplest protocol, already public and already government policy (D1-06-F14, F01 to F04).
- A fast, accepted endpoint: share under 140/90 at 30 to 90 days.
- A safety record at scale for protocol titration by non-physicians (D1-01-F38).
- A visible leak in the existing programme: 51% return (D1-03-F10).
- An open-source incumbent with no step logic (D1-06-F40) and no private service found (D1-04 Digest).

The partner's phrase the mentor reposted fits: the "easiest wedge" into a large market (D2-07-F14).

## 6. What is hard (where the science is)

1. **Trusting a home reading.** Posture, arm position, rest and repeat readings change the number. Home thresholds differ from clinic thresholds, and the Indian protocol is written for clinic readings. The loop needs a rule for when home readings count as "still high" (Round 2, D2-01 and D2-02).
2. **Titrate or support.** Telling a patient who needs a higher dose from one who is not taking the current dose.
3. **A safety case.** Pregnancy, very high readings, lab checks before certain steps, older patients who get dizzy on standing. Every proposal must carry its reasons, and every unsafe case must go up.
4. **Reach.** 63.6% of women own a phone, 57.4% in rural areas (D1-03-F42). The design must work on a shared family phone, with a health worker's help, by voice.
5. **Proof.** A pilot that measures control at 90 days among patients who would otherwise not have returned.

## 7. What would kill it

- Doctors do not approve proposals in time, so the loop stalls.
- Patients do not own or use a cuff.
- The regulator requires a clinical investigation before any pilot.
- Nobody pays.
- It turns out to add nothing over a clinic programme that already works (the mWellcare result, D1-01-F46).

## 8. Expansion (OUR VIEW)

Same loop, new protocol: blood pressure, then insulin and the oral diabetes steps, then heart failure (which needs lab values, so a diagnostics partner), then warfarin and asthma. Each protocol added is a new market with the same engine, the same approval screen and the same safety method.
