**How each figure is counted.**

Times ₹1,788 a year = ₹7,494 crore. The spend figure is a modelled price for one brand pill a day; across the paper's range of about ₹2,500 to ₹5,100 the count runs from 321.0 lakh to 654.8 lakh patients [D2-05-F17].
- **TAM bottom-up.** 315 million adults with high blood pressure (a 2021 projection by the survey's investigators, not a count) [D1-03-F02] x 17.7% on medicines (NFHS-5 2019-21, preprint) [D1-03-F07] = 557.5 lakh (the ceiling from drug sales, 30.7%, a 2018 ceiling that assumes the public sector gives 30% of treatment [D2-05-F30], would give 967.0 lakh). Times ₹1,788 = ₹9,969 crore.
- **SAM bottom-up.** 419.1 lakh private patients x 50% reachable on a household smartphone x 20% whose doctor could enrol them (both ASSUMPTION) = 41.9 lakh patients. Times ₹1,788 = ₹749 crore.

Times ₹1,788 = ₹1,668 crore. The 80% is the ministry's own availability assumption, for allopathic and AYUSH doctors together; the rest are ASSUMPTION. Beside our 70% private: the notes have 11% of registered doctors in government in 2018, and "more than 80%" private in an NSSO-based study (search summary only) [D3-07-F08]. The model has not been rerun with these.

The model leaves out public-system patients (shown in section 6), diabetes and other conditions, device sales and every country outside India.

**The weakest inputs.** The smartphone and enrolment shares (50% and 20%) are guesses, and they set the SAM used; the 50% is in line with smartphone access of 57.9% at ages 50-59 and 44.7% at 60-69 (quoted second-hand) [D3-07-F11]. **So what for us (OUR VIEW).** The used SAM of ₹749 crore is real money but small for a venture fund. The year-5 figure of ₹17.9 crore depends entirely on how fast clinics sign.

### 5. The US$100 million-a-year test

The test asks whether the company could reach US$100 million of revenue a year.

- US$100 million a year = ₹942 crore at ₹94.25 to the dollar (rate on 13 Nov 2026) (OUR ARITHMETIC).
- At ₹1,788 a patient a year that needs 52.7 lakh paying patients (52,71,253) (OUR ARITHMETIC).
- The serviceable market above holds 41.9 lakh patients. **The test fails on this model in India alone**: it needs 1.3 times the SAM (OUR ARITHMETIC).

At higher prices fewer patients are needed: 7,85,417 at ₹1,000 a month, 3,92,708 at ₹2,000, and 3,40,136 at Omada's revenue of about US$24.5 a member a month (revenue over all members, not a price) (OUR ARITHMETIC: ₹9,42,50,00,000 divided by the yearly price) [D2-05-F23].

Where the rest would have to come from: more protocols on the same loop (diabetes, heart failure), a paid public tier, other countries that use the same family of protocols, or a higher price paid by an insurer, employer or medicine maker.

**So what for us (OUR VIEW).** Blood pressure in India is the entry, not the whole company. The pitch must show the same engine carrying more protocols and more payers.

### 10. The rules in India

These are readings of the texts; open points remain questions for a lawyer and for CDSCO (the national medical-device regulator).

**(a) Software as a medical device.** CDSCO issued its "Guidance Document on Medical Device Software" (CDSCO/MD/GD/MDSW/01/2026) on 21 Jul 2026 [D2-04-F01]. Class follows the intended use, as stated in the label, website and promotion [D2-04-F02]. Its grid for standalone software [D2-04-F03]:

| Situation | Treatment or diagnosis | Drive clinical management | Inform clinical management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical | D | C | B |
| Serious | C | B | A |
| Non-serious | B | A | A |

"Drive clinical management" covers information "to guide next diagnostics or next treatment interventions" and support for "safe and effective use of medicinal products" [D2-04-F04]. A dose proposal a doctor approves reads most naturally as "drive": Class B if hypertension is "serious", Class A if "non-serious", Class C if CDSCO reads it as "treatment" [D2-04-F06, D2-04-F49]. The guidance does not say where hypertension sits, so the class turns on that grading [D2-04-F05]. A patient-facing tool used without a professional can be moved up to "critical" [D2-04-F03]. CDSCO names a hypertension digital therapeutics platform and a Bluetooth cuff app as medical device software, and bars the wellness carve-out once a disease is named [D2-04-F07, D2-04-F09]. Unlike the US, there is no exemption for tools only a clinician sees [D2-04-F10]. CDSCO confirms the class on review [D2-04-F06].

**(b) A study on patients before a licence.** A test licence (Form MD-13) covers making small quantities "for the purpose of Clinical Investigations or Test or Evaluation or Demonstration" [D2-04-F13]. A clinical investigation on patients needs CDSCO permission (MD-22/MD-23) [D2-04-F14]; the flowchart notes it is not mandatory for Class A [D2-04-F39]. A desk test on open data, simulators or teammates is not a clinical investigation; whether a pilot where software drafts and a doctor decides counts as one is a question for counsel [D2-04-F50]. Fees and processing days NOT FOUND [D2-04-F16].

**(c) Telemedicine.** The Telemedicine Practice Guidelines (25 Mar 2020), clause 5.4, say platforms based on artificial intelligence or machine learning "are not allowed to counsel the patients or prescribe any medicines"; such tools may "assist and support" the doctor, but only a registered doctor may counsel or prescribe, and "the final prescription or counseling has to be directly delivered by the RMP" (registered medical practitioner) [D2-04-F17]. List B medicines may be added at a follow-up; the guidelines define follow-up as the same doctor, same condition, within 6 months of the previous in-person consultation (clause 3.6.2) [D2-04-F17, D2-01-F10]. Annexure 1 names hypertension examples (List A refills such as enalapril and atenolol; List B add-on of a thiazide diuretic with atenolol); amlodipine, telmisartan and chlorthalidone are not named [D2-04-F17]. The claims check read the official text; whether later rules changed it in 2026 was not checked [D2-04-F17].

**(d) Nurse or health officer changing a dose.** In the national hypertension programme, community health officers delivered refills "based on the patient's prior prescription"; no titration by them is described [D2-04-F18]. NOT FOUND: any law, rule or ministry order letting a nurse, health officer or pharmacist change a dose under a standing order [D2-04-F18].

**(e) Claims and advertising.** The Drugs and Magic Remedies Act 1954 bans advertisements suggesting a drug treats a disease in its Schedule, which lists "Diabetes", "Heart diseases" and "High or low blood pressure" [D2-04-F31]. Whether software counts as a "drug" under it is untested; a question for counsel [D2-04-F31].

**(f) Data law.** Under the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules 2025, consent-manager rules start one year after 13 Nov 2025, on 13 Nov 2026; notice, consent and data-fiduciary duties start 18 months after, on 13 May 2027, by the gazette's wording; one law firm (AZB) says a day earlier, 12 Nov 2026 and 12 May 2027 [D2-04-F19, D2-04-F20]. Health data has no general exemption; only children's-data duties are waived for health providers [D2-04-F40].

**(g) Ethics and registration.** ICMR's 2023 AI guidelines say humans should keep "complete control" and require consent that names the AI [D2-04-F35]. Trial registration on CTRI is mandatory before first enrolment for trials, with ethics approval uploaded; whether a clinician-decides pilot needs it is NOT FOUND (search summary only) [D2-04-F45].

**So what for us (OUR VIEW).** Clinician sign-off lowers the class; it does not take the software out of the device rules.

### 12. What tonight's prototype may and may not say

OUR VIEW, drawn from section 10:
- It is a prototype for test and demonstration, not a medical device on sale and not tested on patients [D2-04-F50].
- It uses open data, simulated patients or teammates' readings; no patient data.
- A clinician approves every dose change and the doctor directly delivers the prescription and counselling; the software only drafts and shows its reasons [D2-04-F17, D2-04-F35].
- It makes no claim that it treats or controls high blood pressure, and no claim of benefit to patients [D2-04-F31].

### 13. Three questions for a lawyer

From the regulation note [D2-04-F49, D2-04-F50]:
1. Under CDSCO's 2026 grid, is a clinician-approved blood-pressure (or insulin) step proposal "drive clinical management" or "treatment or diagnosis", and is essential hypertension "serious" or "non-serious"? Will CDSCO give a written class opinion before we build?
2. For a pilot where software drafts and a registered doctor approves each change, do we need a test licence, clinical-investigation permission and CTRI registration, or only ethics approval? Who is the sponsor and the data fiduciary: the clinic, us, or both?
3. Under the 2020 telemedicine guidelines and the prescription-drug rules, may an approved change be sent by text as a follow-up consultation, does a message sent in the doctor's name after approval count as the doctor directly delivering the prescription (clause 5.4), and may a nurse or health officer deliver it under a written protocol? Does marketing that names hypertension engage section 3(d) of the 1954 Act?

### 14. So what for us

OUR VIEW:
- Sell to private clinics and hospital chains first, at about ₹149 a patient a month (ASSUMPTION); patients alone will not pay enough.
- The used SAM is ₹749 crore and the US$100 million test fails in India on blood pressure alone (OUR ARITHMETIC), so the pitch is one engine for many protocols and payers.
